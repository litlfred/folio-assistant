automatic-term-extraction — declared by cat-harness — source held

Applies when. Finding which words and phrases in a body of prose are the TERMS of its domain, so that a glossary can be proposed from the text rather than typed from memory. It answers which strings are candidate terms, ranked. Every output is a candidate: a person decides what is a term and writes its definition (glossary-terms: an extractor writes candidate, only a person moves one to authored). Not for assigning terms from a closed controlled vocabulary (skill-pipeline-subject-indexing), not for keywords that summarise one document against the others (lsi, correspondence-analysis, the keyword graph), and not for reading definitions a source already states in its own glossary section, which are copied verbatim and need no extraction.

Origin. Automatic term extraction (ATE) as surveyed by Hanh Thi Hong Tran, Matej Martinc, Jaya Caporusso, Antoine Doucet and Senja Pollak, “The Recent Advances in Automatic Term Extraction: A survey” (arXiv:2301.06767v1, CC BY 4.0), ingested whole and read. The C-value measure for nested multi-word units is Katerina T. Frantzi and Sophia Ananiadou, “Extracting Nested Collocations”, COLING 1996 (ACL Anthology C96-1009, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0), ingested whole and read; its equations were checked against the printed page, because the Anthology’s own text layer is a poor OCR. The later C-value/NC-value papers that apply the measure to terms (Frantzi, Ananiadou & Tsujii 1998; Frantzi, Ananiadou & Mima 2000) are not open access and are not held.

The task, in the survey’s words

“ATE systems usually employ a two-step procedure: (1) extracting a list of candidate terms, and (2) determining which candidate terms are correct using supervised or unsupervised techniques.” (§1)

The candidates come from preprocessing (tokenisation, lemmatisation, POS tagging, chunking). The second step scores them with features from six families (§5.1, Fig. 2): shape, linguistic, frequency, statistical, contextual and variational. The statistical family splits in two. Some measures need no reference corpus, such as C-value, TF-IDF and lexical cohesion. Others compare the domain text with a general one, such as weirdness, domain relevance and termhood.

C-value, as Frantzi & Ananiadou define it (§3)

C-value scores a candidate string a by how often it occurs, how long it is, and how often it occurs inside longer candidates. It exists for nested units: a phrase such as “Wall Street” that occurs mostly inside longer ones (“Wall Street Journal”) and would score low on its own frequency.

caseC-value(a)
a has the same frequency as a longer candidate that contains it (it only ever occurs inside that one)0: not a candidate
a is not a substring of any extracted candidate(|a| − 1) · n(a) (eq. 2)
a occurs inside one or more longer candidates, not always the same one(|a| − 1) · (n(a) − t(a) / c(a)) (eq. 3)

n(a) is the frequency of a, |a| its length in words, t(a) its total frequency inside longer candidates, and c(a) the number of those longer candidates. The algorithm visits n-grams from the longest down, after a minimum frequency cut-off, and revises t and c of every substring as it goes. The more different longer candidates contain a, the smaller t(a)/c(a) and the higher its C-value.

Two limits the paper states itself. It measured no precision or recall, because those “depend on the definition of collocation and they are domain dependent” (§4). And it presents the method as semi-automatic: “It is the human judge that will give the final decision” (§3). The 1996 paper is about collocations; applying it to terms is its own stated future work (§6), done in the papers that are not held.

How a result is judged (survey §6)

Three evaluation methods: human judges, a dictionary, or a gold standard list. Three scopes: all results, part of them, or the top k. With a gold standard the measures are precision, recall and F1. The survey notes that “the performance of different approaches is often not directly comparable” because the setups differ.

Here, the gold standard for a layer is its own authored glossary terms: precision is the share of candidates a person accepted, and recall is the share of authored terms that the extractor proposed.

Nested terms are the hard case (survey §8.1)

Nested terms are “words or phrases that are both terms themselves and parts of longer terms”. The most common error the survey reports for current systems is that they predict only the shorter, nested term instead of the whole multi-word term, or the other way round. This is the case C-value was built for, and it is why C-value is the statistical measure this node names.

What this platform adopts, and what it refuses

Adopted:

  • the two steps, candidates then scoring;
  • C-value as the first scoring measure, because it needs no reference corpus and it addresses nested terms;
  • evaluation against the layer’s authored terms, reported as precision and recall, and never as one score.

Refused:

  1. An extracted term is never a definition. It enters the glossary as candidate, with no definition until a person writes one. This is the 1996 paper’s human judge, and it is glossary-terms’ existing rule.
  2. A score is not a decision. The ranking orders the review queue. A cut-off may shorten the queue, but it never promotes a term.
  3. A measure the sources do not define is not added under this node’s name. NC-value’s context weights, or the log-length variant of C-value, come in with their own held source or not at all.

How it is performed here

The glossary skills that read prose for terms name this node in their uses-methodology, and the Tool that computes C-value satisfies them. Both live in the content layer, which links down to here. The Tool’s test reproduces Table 2 of the 1996 paper. By default it applies the paper’s method as published, which on ordinary prose ranks function phrases such as “it is” first (the paper: many candidates “may seem unimportant”, §4).
