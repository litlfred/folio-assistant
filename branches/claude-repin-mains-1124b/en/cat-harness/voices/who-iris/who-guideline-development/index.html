How a WHO recommendation is worded and what must travel with it: strength, certainty, justification, remarks and a link to the evidence. Derived from the WHO handbook for guideline development, 2nd edition. This voice governs the language of normative statements, not the process that produces them — the process is the handbook's subject and belongs in a workflow, not a style rule.

who-gdl-strength-verbs"should" is strong; "suggest" or "consider" is conditional
GRADE's wording convention, adopted by the handbook: a strong recommendation uses "should" or "strongly recommend"; a conditional one uses "suggest" or "consider". The two vocabularies must not be mixed, because the verb is how a reader learns the strength — a conditional recommendation phrased with "should" reads as strong and will be implemented as one.		methodology · critical
GRADE recommends using terms or phrases such as "should" or "strongly recommend" for strong recommendations, and "suggest" or "consider" for conditional ones.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131
who-gdl-no-not-recommendedNever "not recommended" — say "we recommend against X"
The handbook singles this phrase out as ambiguous, and names both readings: it can mean that no recommendation either way was made, or that the intervention should not be implemented. Where the second is meant, the required wording is "we recommend against intervention X…". This is the one rule in the voice whose severity is about reader safety rather than register: the two readings lead to opposite clinical actions.		methodology · critical
The use of confusing or ambiguous phrases should be avoided. For example, the phrase "not recommended" could be interpreted to mean either that no recommendation for or against the intervention has been made, or that the intervention should not be implemented. In the latter instance, "we recommend against intervention X…" is the preferred wording.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131
who-gdl-active-voiceRecommendations are written in the active voice, and worded consistently
The handbook asks for the active voice and for consistency across every recommendation in one guideline. Consistency is the harder half and the one a per-block check cannot see: it is a property of the set, so a reviewer must read the recommendations together rather than one at a time.		register · major
The language of each recommendation is critically important. Wherever possible, it should be consistent across all recommendations in a guideline, which should be written in the active voice.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131
who-gdl-no-outcomes-in-the-statementKeep outcomes out of the recommendation itself
Naming an outcome in the recommendation implies that outcome was the only one considered. The handbook asks that outcomes generally stay out of the statement — they belong in the evidence summary and the remarks. A recommendation that reads "…to reduce mortality" is the shape to question.		structure · major
Outcomes should generally not be mentioned in the recommendation to avoid the impression that only single outcomes are relevant or were considered.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131
who-gdl-pico-and-actionableClear, actionable, and in PICO shape
A recommendation reflects the PICO format — population, intervention, comparator, outcome — and is actionable: a reader must be able to tell who it applies to and what to do. Combined with the rule above, the resolution is that P, I and C appear in the statement while O informs it without being named.		structure · major
Recommendations need to be clear and actionable, reflect the PICO format and contain an indication of their strength and of the quality of the evidence on which they are based.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131
who-gdl-certainty-four-levelsState the certainty as high, moderate, low or very low
Four levels, named exactly. A recommendation with no stated certainty is incomplete, and so is one that grades the evidence on some other scale — "strong evidence" is not a GRADE level and conflates certainty with strength, which the handbook keeps apart throughout.		methodology · major
The quality of the underlying body of evidence (high, moderate, low or very low) should be specified.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131
who-gdl-justification-and-remarksEvery recommendation carries its justification, its remarks and a link to the evidence
Four things travel with the statement: why it is strong or conditional, why it is for or against, remarks giving the conditions and context in which it applies and what to bear in mind when implementing it, and a link to the evidence — a systematic review, the GRADE evidence profiles and the evidence-to-decision tables. A block carrying the recommendation and none of these is not yet a recommendation; in a folio this is what `uses[]` and the evidence sidecar are for.		structure · major
A recommendation should include a justification as to why it is strong or conditional and why it is for or against a given intervention. It should also contain a set of remarks explaining the conditions and context in which the recommendation applies and the points to bear in mind regarding implementation. … Each recommendation should be linked to a summary of the evidence (e.g. a published systematic review or the systematic review in an online annex), the GRADE evidence profiles and the evidence-to-decision tables.
9789241548960-eng#sec-180-106-writing-recommendations, p131-132
who-gdl-conditional-states-its-conditionsA conditional recommendation names the conditions
The handbook's account of why a recommendation is conditional — the GDG being less certain about the balance of benefits against harms — carries a drafting consequence: a conditional recommendation generally includes a description of the conditions under which the end-user should or should not implement it. So "we suggest X" with no conditions attached has the form of a conditional recommendation and not the content.		structure · major
Recommendations that are conditional or weak are made when a GDG is less certain about the balance between the benefits and harms or disadvantages of implementing a recommendation. Conditional recommendations generally include a description of the conditions under which the end-user should or should not implement the recommendation.
9789241548960-eng#sec-177-1042-conditional-or-weak-recommendations, p129
