crdm — declared by cat-harness — source held

Applies when. Eliciting, structuring, prioritising, and validating functional and non-functional requirements from current-state and redesigned business processes before implementation begins. Use it when a capability, specification, or workflow spans actors and tasks that must be analysed and redesigned first so every requirement traces to a concrete business activity. Not a replacement for filing and lifecycle governance (requirements-filing-and-organisation), interchange standards mapping (requirements-standards-assessment), one-off option trade-offs (kepner-tregoe), recurring decision logic (dmn), or architecture decision records (madr).

1. The PHII and PATH CRDM method

PHII developed CRDM as a process-centric methodology grounded in business process management that brings multi-disciplinary subject matter experts (SMEs) together to document how work is performed, redesign how it should be performed, and derive what an information system must do to support that work (phii-2023-crdm, pp. 1–3; path-2012-common-requirements-mhis-crdm, pp. 4–7).

1.1 The three concentration areas

PHII organises CRDM around three sequential concentration areas (phii-2023-crdm, pp. 2–9):

Concentration areaGuiding promptCore artefactsWhat happens
1. Business Process Analysis (BPA)Think: How do we do our work now?Business Process Matrix (process goal, inputs, activities, outputs, actors/roles, business rules, issues/opportunities) and Task Flow Diagrams (swimlane diagrams showing the sequence of tasks across pools and actors)SMEs map current-state operations end to end, making implicit workflows, hand-offs, and decision points explicit (phii-2023-crdm, pp. 3–6).
2. Business Process Redesign (BPR)Rethink: How should we do our work?Redesigned Business Process Matrix and updated Task Flow DiagramsBefore automating anything, participants examine the current-state model to eliminate bottlenecks, redundancies, policy barriers, and information gaps (phii-2023-crdm, pp. 7–8).
3. Requirements DefinitionDescribe: How can an information system support our work?Functional User Requirements (anchored to numbered task-flow activities) and Non-Functional / General System RequirementsEvery activity in the redesigned task flow is examined to state the information system capabilities needed to support it, followed by structured SME validation (phii-2023-crdm, pp. 8–9; phii-2021-defining-and-validating-requirements-guide, pp. 1–5).

1.2 PATH (2012): domain-scale health information system artefacts

PATH & PHII (2012) demonstrate how CRDM scales across a clinical and public health domain (path-2012-common-requirements-mhis-crdm):

  1. Process Framework (Table 1, PDF p. 8): Inventories all domain business processes across functional columns (Patient Administration, Patient Care, Patient Fees and Accounting, Planning, Management & Surveillance) and selects priority processes for deep modeling (sec-009-outputs-of-the-crdm-process.md).
  2. Task Flow Diagrams with Activity Narratives (Appendix 1, PDF pp. 11–20): Draws each priority process using a ten-element notation (Pools, Swim Lanes, Start Event, End Event, Sequence Flow, Activity, Subprocess, Decision, Multi-Page Connector, Annotation) paired with numbered activity details and narratives (sec-013-appendix-1-business-process-flows.md).
  3. Activity-Linked Functional User Requirements vs. General System Requirements (Appendices 2–3, PDF pp. 21–36): Groups functional requirements by process and numbered activity ID, and separates cross-cutting system requirements (security, audit logging, reporting, interoperability, offline operation) into a dedicated general system requirements catalogue (sec-014-appendix-2-requirements.md, which also holds the general and nonfunctional requirements).

1.3 Quality criteria and validation techniques

PHII’s Defining and Validating System Requirements Guide (phii-2021-defining-and-validating-requirements-guide, pp. 3–5) checks every requirement against nine quality criteria — Correct, Unambiguous, Complete, Consistent, Ranked for importance and/or stability, Verifiable, Modifiable, Traceable, and Comprehensible — using three validation methods:

  1. Desk checking: Individual review of the specification against the business process models and quality criteria.
  2. Walkthrough: Facilitated step-by-step group traversal of task flows and requirements with the SMEs who perform the work.
  3. Peer review: Independent structured inspection by peers to catch ambiguities, omissions, and conflicts before sign-off.

2. How the harness adapts PHII and PATH CRDM

The relation between crdm-requirements-workflow and PHII/PATH CRDM is adapts: Phases 1–3 and the SME validation walkthrough follow PHII/PATH CRDM directly, while the facilitation channel, Phase 4/4b codebase impact and dependency analysis, Phase 5 work-item and filing decomposition, and Phase 6 pull-request sign-off are the platform’s own extensions.

3. Priority scale and normative conformance keywords

In CRDM and in the harness’s requirement template (crdm-requirements-template), implementation priority and normative conformance level are two distinct fields with different semantics and different standards origins:

3.1 Priority weighting (High / Medium / Low = Must-have / Should-have / Nice-to-have)

PHII’s Defining and Validating System Requirements Guide (phii-2021-defining-and-validating-requirements-guide, p. 2) and PATH (2012) (path-2012-common-requirements-mhis-crdm, p. 1 / Appendix 2) rank requirements by relative implementation importance using a three-tier priority scale. The harness aligns its three priority labels one-to-one with PHII’s scale:

PHII / PATH priority rankHarness template labelMeaning when sequencing or trading off scope
HighMust-have (High / Must-have)Essential to the core business process; the system cannot satisfy the process goal without this capability.
MediumShould-have (Medium / Should-have)Important for operational efficiency or completeness; expected in standard operation, though a temporary workaround may exist.
LowNice-to-have (Low / Nice-to-have)Desirable enhancement that improves convenience or secondary workflows when resources permit.

3.2 Normative conformance keywords (SHALL / SHOULD / MAY / SHALL NOT)

While PHII’s informal prose grammar phrases requirement statements as “The system must or should…” (phii-2021-defining-and-validating-requirements-guide, p. 1), the harness’s separate Conformance field (SHALL / MUST, SHOULD, MAY, SHALL NOT / MUST NOT) uses uppercase normative keywords governed by IETF BCP 14:

  • RFC 2119 (Bradner 1997, library/rfc2119-key-words-requirement-levels): Defines MUST / SHALL (absolute requirement of the specification), MUST NOT / SHALL NOT (absolute prohibition), SHOULD (recommended; valid reasons may exist in particular circumstances to ignore, but full implications must be understood and weighed), SHOULD NOT (not recommended), and MAY (truly optional).
  • RFC 8174 (Leiba 2017, library/rfc8174-uppercase-vs-lowercase-2119-key-words): Clarifies that only UPPERCASE words carry the RFC 2119 normative meanings; lowercase “must”, “should”, and “may” in surrounding prose carry only their normal English meaning.

Keeping Priority (High / Must-have, Medium / Should-have, Low / Nice-to-have) separate from Conformance (SHALL, SHOULD, MAY, SHALL NOT) avoids conflating when a capability is scheduled for delivery (priority) with how strictly an implementation must behave once it claims conformance to that requirement (BCP 14 modality).
