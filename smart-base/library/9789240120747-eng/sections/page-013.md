Introduction
Substandard and falsified medical products pose 
a multilayered threat to the stability of health 
systems. They endanger health, through either 
therapeutic failure or adverse effects, and they 
undermine public trust in health care. They 
also risk jeopardizing global efforts working to 
suppress antimicrobial resistance, threatening the 
long-term efficacy of key life-saving medicines. 
Further to their impacts on health, substandard 
and falsified medical products have adverse 
socioeconomic consequences and increase 
poverty by damaging individual productivity, 
income and social mobility. These factors lead to 
wasted resources and increased out-of-pocket 
spending. Economic losses compound the health 
impacts of substandard and falsified medical 
products, completing a downward spiral of 
negative effects (22).
WHO and Member States have agreed on the 
following definitions (23):
“Substandard medical products”, 
also called “out-of-specification”, are 
authorized medical products that fail to 
meet either their quality standards or 
their specifications, or both. 
“Falsified medical products” deliberately 
and or fraudulently misrepresent their 
identity, composition or source.
With globalization, opportunities for substandard 
and falsified medical products to enter the 
market and formal supply chain are increasing. 
A 2017 estimate, based on multiple studies over 
the previous decade, suggested that one in ten 
medical products in low- and middle-income 
countries (LMICs) could be substandard and 
falsified (24). The growing complexity and online 
presence in supply chains further obscure the 
entry points of falsified products. As LMICs 
transition to universal health coverage, the 
demand for medicines from public sector and 
insured patients increases (25). In some cases, 
government procurement measures aim to 
contain costs and reduce medicine prices by 
procuring medicines at higher volumes (26). 
Reduced prices due to higher volume spending 
(i.e. restricted pricing) negatively affect the 
incentives of pharmaceutical companies, which 
may lead to cost-cutting and underinvestment 
in quality assurance, increasing the risk of 
substandard products; or encouraging companies 
to withdraw from the market, providing 
opportunities for falsifiers to enter (26). 
Detection of these products in circulation is one of 
the key steps required to reduce their prevalence. 
However, most substandard and falsified medical 
products can only be identified by professionals 
with the use of laboratory technologies (27). 
In cases where resources are limited, and 
technology is not widespread, risk-based post-
market surveillance (Rb-PMS) of medical products 
becomes essential for identifying suspect 
products. Unlike conventional post-market 
surveillance (PMS), Rb-PMS focuses on prioritizing 
and allocating resources based on the level of risk 
associated with products. Once identified, these 
products can be further investigated by national 
medicines regulatory authorities (NMRAs), 
in collaboration with the police or other law 
enforcement agencies if there is an indication of 
possible criminal activity.
Reporting of substandard and 
falsified medical products
Reporting suspected substandard and falsified 
medical products allows NMRAs to investigate 
them more thoroughly than the reporter. When 
a case of a substandard and falsified medical 
product is confirmed, relevant authorities can 
quickly remove the product from the supply 
chain and market, impose sanctions and address 
system weaknesses. In the long term, this should 
improve product quality and reduce the chances 
of substandard and falsified medical products 
entering the market (27).
In addition to reducing prevalence at a national 
level, NMRAs can report data on substandard and 
falsified medical products to WHO’s case reporting 
system for substandard and falsified medical 
products – the WHO Global Surveillance and 
Monitoring System (GSMS) – to facilitate global 
action on the issue. Effective global coordination 
against these products requires high-quality and 
frequent reporting and data exchange.
Health care professionals can play an important 
role in reporting products they suspect of being 
ineffective or defective, in turn increasing the 
capacity of NMRAs to successfully identify 
substandard and falsified products. Health 
care professionals are ideally placed to notice 
suspicious products as they are at the frontline of 
health care and able to spot therapeutic failure
