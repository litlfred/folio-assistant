Digital transformation handbook for reporting substandard and falsified medical products
2.2 Align policy with stakeholders
Tackling substandard and falsified medical 
products requires more than a single institution. 
Effective policy depends on multisectoral 
collaboration across:
•	
Law enforcement and customs. Align 
policies to enable swift data sharing with 
customs officers and law enforcement, who 
often spearhead investigations and border 
control.
•	
Health financing and procurement 
agencies. Support policy coordination with 
procurement bodies and insurance systems to 
detect falsified products within supply chains 
or reimbursement claims.
•	
Innovation and ICT ministries. Collaborate 
with digital innovation and ICT agencies 
to integrate the reporting system with 
existing national eHealth strategies or digital 
infrastructure.
•	
Regional and global regulatory authorities. 
Work to harmonize regulations and support 
national authorities, e.g. European Medicines 
Agency, African Medicines Agency or WHO.
2.3 Establish governance and 
leadership
Establish a robust governance structure for 
coordinating the multiple stakeholders involved 
in substandard and falsified medical product 
reporting. Define clear roles and responsibilities 
to avoid duplication of effort, enhance 
transparency and create a stable platform for 
decision-making.
A governance structure often includes:
•	
National steering committee. Led by the 
ministry of health or NMRA, this committee 
provides strategic oversight, ensures 
alignment with national health goals and 
mobilizes resources. The national steering 
committee drives and makes key strategic 
decisions on substandard and falsified 
medical products reporting. 
•	
Technical working group. Comprised of 
subject matter experts (e.g. medical product 
regulators, IT specialists, pharmacovigilance 
experts), working under the leadership of a 
steering committee, the technical working 
group would be responsible for the design, 
planning and implementation details. 
•	
Advisory council. External experts, 
professional associations, patient advocacy 
groups and private sector partners can offer 
insights on best practices, user expectations 
and emerging technologies to the national 
steering committee and/or technical working 
group. However, they do not have any 
decision-making ability. 
As part of the overall governance structure, strong 
leadership is critical for sustaining momentum 
and ensuring accountability across the following 
stakeholders:
•	
Ministry of health/NMRA. Responsible for 
providing political support and securing 
funding to shape regulatory policies that 
guide digital reporting and data governance.
•	
Project champion. Typically, a high-level 
official or recognized expert who can 
advocate for the project across government 
and partners. They engage health care 
professional associations, civil society and 
private sector players such as manufacturers 
and distributors and are responsible for 
mobilizing stakeholders to obtain buy-in and 
address policy barriers. 
•	
Digital health coordinator/project 
manager. Responsible for overseeing daily 
implementation tasks and engaging key 
stakeholders. They ensure alignment with 
the national architectural blueprint and 
roadmap, maintaining quality and timeliness 
of deliverables. They facilitate partnerships, 
communication campaigns and user feedback 
loops.
2.4 Set the strategic goals
Define strategic goals for the substandard and 
falsified reporting application so that it is built to 
appropriately address prioritized needs. These 
goals should be:
•	
Specific. For instance, reduce the average 
detection-to-notification time for suspected 
substandard and falsified medical products 
from 14 days to 2 days.
•	
Measurable. Use indicators like the number 
of detected substandard and falsified 
incidents, time to regulatory action, or user 
engagement metrics in the digital reporting 
platform.
•	
Achievable. Align goals with available 
resources and capacity. Overly ambitious 
targets can undermine stakeholder 
confidence if they are continually missed.
•	
Relevant. Ensure every goal ties directly to 
better patient safety, stronger regulatory 
action or improved supply chain transparency.
•	
Time-bound. Assign deadlines or milestones 
to each goal (e.g. 12 months post-launch, 24 
months post-launch).
