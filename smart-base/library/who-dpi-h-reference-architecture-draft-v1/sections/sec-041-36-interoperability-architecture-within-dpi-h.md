The delivery of healthcare involves many actors — ministries and their programmes, public and 
723 
private facilities, clinicians and community health workers, laboratories and pharmacies, supply 
724 
chain operators, health coverage schemes, researchers, and the people receiving care — each 
725 
holding part of the information another needs, and each with its own informational requirements. 
726 
No single system can hold all of this, and none should attempt to. Interoperability is the property 
727 
that allows these distinct systems to share information through common standards and agreed 
728 
 
 
72 
exchange mechanisms, so that data, wherever it is accessed across the health ecosystem, is 
729 
timely, secure, clinically meaningful and usable for the purpose at hand.26 
730 
 
731 
Interoperability, therefore, is a cross-cutting capability of the reference architecture as a whole, 
732 
rather than a single component a country procures or a discrete layer it installs. It is realised 
733 
through several complementary things working together: shared content and terminology 
734 
standards; governed information models; agreed exchange patterns; and the foundational trust 
735 
and transport services on which exchange depends. It is helpful to distinguish the recognised 
736 
dimensions along which interoperability is achieved in healthcare — the technical ability to move 
737 
data between systems; the structural consistency of how that data is organised; the semantic 
738 
preservation of what the data means; and the organisational and governance arrangements, 
739 
including legal basis, consent and data-sharing agreements, that make exchange lawful and 
740 
trusted.27 Technical exchange without structural, semantic and organisational interoperability 
741 
moves data that the receiving system is unable to interpret or act upon safely. This section 
742 
therefore addresses all of these dimensions rather than transport alone.
