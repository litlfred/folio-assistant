Closely related to readiness is the question of what makes AI trustworthy in a health setting. 
155 
Trustworthy AI is not a quality a product can assert for itself; it is earned through evidence that a 
156 
system is effective for its stated purpose, safe, transparent about its limitations, accountable to 
157 
identifiable people, fair across the populations it serves, and subject to meaningful human 
158 
oversight. WHO's guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health sets out consensus 
159 
principles along these lines44, and its guidance on large multi-modal models extends them to 
160 
generative systems45. A note of caution is warranted. Organisations are frequently approached 
161 
with AI offerings that promise broad benefits, and this guidance is intended to help decision-
162 
makers interrogate such claims — asking what specific task a system performs, what data it 
163 
depends on, how it was evaluated, who is accountable when it errs, and how its use is to be 
164 
governed — rather than to encourage adoption for its own sake. More concretely, before a 
165 
capability is relied upon, and at intervals thereafter, it should be evaluated against representative 
166 
cases and explicit criteria, a discipline increasingly referred to as evaluation, or evals, so that its 
167 
behaviour is understood and monitored rather than assumed. WHO maintains further resources 
168 
on the responsible use of AI for health that countries can draw on48.
