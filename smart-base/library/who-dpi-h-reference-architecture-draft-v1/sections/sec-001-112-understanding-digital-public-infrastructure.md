According to the World Bank Group6, DPI is an approach to digitalization focused on creating 
35 
“foundational, digital building blocks designed for the public benefit.” They comprise a variety of 
36 
digital software, platforms, APIs, and services, along with their related legal and regulatory 
37 
frameworks, standards, policies, and processes.7 Critically, by providing essential digital 
38 
functions at society scale that can be reused across sectors, DPIs enable public and private 
39 
service providers to build on these systems, innovate, and roll out new services more quickly 
40 
and efficiently. 
41 
Building on this understanding, the G207 described DPI as a set of shared digital systems that 
42 
should be secure and interoperable, and can be built on open standards and specifications to 
43 
deliver and provide equitable access to public and/or private services at societal scale and are 
44 
governed by applicable legal frameworks and enabling rules to drive development, inclusion, 
45 
innovation, trust, and competition, while respecting human rights and fundamental freedoms. 
46 
Taken together, these definitions establish foundational characteristics of DPI: that it is shared 
47 
infrastructure operating at societal scale, and that its value derives not from any single system 
48 
but from its open, interoperable nature upon which both public and private actors can build. The 
49 
G20 itself recognises that DPI is an evolving concept, not necessarily limited to systems with 
50 
these characteristics, and one that may be tailored to specific country contexts and referred to 
51 
using different terminologies.7 This provides the basis for articulating a health-specific 
52 
understanding of DPI, adapted to the needs of the health sector while remaining consistent with 
53 
the broader consensus. It is from this cross-sectoral understanding that health-specific digital 
54 
public infrastructure is derived. 
55 
 
Health Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI-H) refers to digital resources that are built as a 
shared means for fulfilling multiple health agency functions. 
 
 
56 
These digital resources for health — encompassing both systems and specifications — 
57 
leverage foundational DPI, are interoperable, reusable, publicly governed and built on open 
58 
standards. They enable countries to autonomously manage the secure processing, collection, 
59 
exchange, and use of health-related data to deliver essential health services and fulfil core 
60 
health sector functions, while upholding individual data rights and ensuring equitable access.

Two dimensions of this definition warrant particular attention. The first is the emphasis on 
62 
shared means. DPI-H is not a collection of programme-specific tools or point-of-service 
63 
applications; it is infrastructure that is shared, reusable, and available to all actors within the 
64 
health system without requiring each to build equivalent capabilities independently. The second 
65 
is the relationship between DPI-H and foundational DPI. DPI-H does not seek to replicate what 
66 
foundational DPI provides; rather, it extends and complements them with the domain-specific 
67 
capabilities that the health sector requires but that general-purpose DPI cannot accommodate. 
68 
Where foundational DPI exists and is accessible, DPI-H builds upon it. Where it does not, DPI-H 
69 
provides the essential capabilities the health system needs to function while remaining 
70 
architecturally open to integration as the broader digital ecosystem matures. 
71 
Together, these two dimensions — shared infrastructure and purposeful extension of 
72 
foundational DPI — define what distinguishes DPI-H from the vertical, programme-driven 
73 
systems, functional applications, and point-of-service solutions that have historically 
74 
characterised health sector digitalisation. Where those approaches have delivered value within 
75 
their individual contexts, they have done so at the cost of fragmentation, duplication, and limited 
76 
interoperability across the health system as a whole. DPI-H establishes a different foundation: 
77 
shared, reusable, and governed infrastructure upon which programmes, applications, and 
78 
services can be built to fulfil multiple health agency functions, without recreating the capabilities 
79 
that should be common to all. It is this architectural logic that underpins the reference 
80 
architecture presented in this guidance document.
