The seven priority health goals that underpin this reference architecture were systematically 
453 
mapped to the digital capabilities and foundational components required to achieve them. This 
454 
mapping is the methodological backbone of the architecture, and it provides the consolidated 
455 
account of how goals translate into component choices. 
456 
The architecture was designed from the perspective of what the health system needs to 
457 
achieve, rather than from the perspective of what technologies exist. This matters because a 
458 
goals-first approach differs from technology-led design. Technology-led design begins with the 
459 
technologies or components available and works outward to what they enable; a goals-first 
460 
approach begins from the health outcomes a country is accountable for and works toward the 
461 
shared infrastructure those goals require. While capability-based, goals-driven approaches are 
462 
established in enterprise architecture, their rigorous application to national-scale digital health

infrastructure — deriving components from health-system goals rather than from a catalogue of 
464 
technologies — is a meaningful departure from how such architectures have often been 
465

466 
The mapping was conducted using a TOGAF-based approach, working top-down from health 
467 
goals through stakeholder value, business capabilities, application services, and application 
468 
components, while simultaneously working bottom-up from known DPI-H components and 
469 
existing digital health infrastructure. The convergence of these two directions produced a 
470 
requirements picture grounded in both what countries need to achieve and what is 
471 
implementable through shared infrastructure, keeping the architecture simultaneously 
472 
aspirational and realistic. 
473 
A consistent finding across all seven goals is that the same foundational components recur. The 
474 
client registry, for example, is required across trusted personal health records, quality and 
475 
continuity of care, public health and disease surveillance, and health financing; the facility 
476 
registry is equally cross-cutting. This convergence is what distinguishes DPI-H components 
477 
from programme-specific tools, and it confirms not just which components belong in shared 
478 
infrastructure but why they warrant investment as such. 
479 
Equally consistent is that these shared components do not, on their own, deliver the outcome. 
480 
The outcome is delivered when point-of-service and functional applications — the systems 
481 
clinicians, programme managers, and administrators actually use — draw on that shared 
482 
infrastructure to do the work. The architecture defines and governs the shared components; it 
483 
enables the applications that consume them rather than defining those applications. Each goal 
484 
therefore depends on both: shared infrastructure for consistency and reuse, and the 
485 
applications that turn it into delivered services.
