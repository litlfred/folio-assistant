The dominant pattern in health system digital transformation to date has been vertical and 
275 
programme-driven, and its costs are now well understood. HIV programmes have built their own 
276 
patient tracking systems, immunisation programmes have built their own registries, maternal 
277 
health, TB, and malaria programmes have each acquired dedicated platforms, each with their 
278 
own patient identification logic, their own facility lists, their own data formats, and their own 
279 
integration requirements. The result in a lot of LMICs is a fragmented landscape of parallel 
280 
systems that are expensive to maintain, difficult to interconnect, and that place a compounding 
281 
burden on the health workers and facilities that need to operate them in parallel.  
282 
 
283 
The economic cost of this fragmentation is not linear — it grows quadratically. Without shared 
284 
infrastructure, every programme that needs to exchange data with another would be required to 
285 
build and maintain its own direct connection to each of the others. Adding a single new 
286 
programme means adding a new connection to every existing one and each integration would 
287 
have to be built, tested, funded, and maintained. The cost of keeping a fragmented ecosystem 
288 
connected compounds with every system added and in a health system with many active 
289 
programmes, the cumulative number of connections that have to be built, funded, and 
290 
 
 
18 
maintained becomes unmanageable — and every time any one system is updated, every 
291 
connection to it is at risk. 
292 
 
293 
However, understanding why fragmentation persists is as important as understanding its costs. 
294 
This was elaborated in a recent report from a high-level dialogue that sought to understand how 
295 
countries can sustainably leverage DPI to transform health systems11. The report highlighted 
296 
that the dominant pattern of vertical, programme-driven digitisation is not simply the result of 
297 
poor planning or lack of awareness, rather it reflects a set of structural incentives that actively 
298 
work against shared infrastructure. The report outlined issues like career trajectories within 
299 
health programmes rewarding delivery against programme-specific targets, procurement 
300 
systems being designed around discrete project cycles with defined outputs, and donor funding 
301 
structures creating accountability to individual programme results rather than to the health 
302 
system as a whole. In this environment, departments and programmes are consistently better 
303 
rewarded for protecting and developing their own systems than for contributing to shared 
304 
infrastructure that benefits others. Therefore, coordination is at its core, a political economy 
305 
problem as much as a technical one. 
306 
Nevertheless, this does not diminish the case for DPI-H but rather clarifies the nature of the 
307 
challenge. Countries and their partners that choose to pursue shared infrastructure are not 
308 
simply making a technical decision; they are choosing to work against incentive structures that 
309 
favour the status quo. The value of DPI-H is therefore not only the efficiency and interoperability 
310 
it delivers once in place, but the deliberate political and institutional commitment it represents — 
311 
a choice to prioritise long-term health system coherence over the short-term convenience of 
312 
vertical approaches, accepting a larger upfront investment in exchange for compounding, 
313 
system-wide returns.
