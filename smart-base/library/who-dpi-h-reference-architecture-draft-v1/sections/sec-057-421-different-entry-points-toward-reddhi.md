Countries arrive at DPI-H implementation from different starting positions. Rather than 
65 
prescribing a single implementation sequence, this guidance identifies four common entry points 
66 
and describes how each advance toward REDDHI (Resilient Essential Data and Digital Health 
67 
Infrastructure). Countries should identify which entry point most closely reflects their current 
68 
reality and adapt the guidance accordingly. 
69 
 
70 
Entry point 1 — Building from the ground up.  
71 
Countries with limited existing digital health infrastructure and early-stage foundational DPI face 
72 
the challenge of establishing shared foundations from scratch. The risk here is that donor-
73 
 
 
90 
funded projects move faster than national governance, creating new silos before shared 
74 
infrastructure is in place. The priority for these countries is to establish governance and 
75 
architectural standards before major technology investments are made, so that every 
76 
subsequent investment conforms to a coherent national architecture rather than fragmenting it 
77 
further. The REDDHI minimum viable layer then provides the sequenced starting point. 
78 
Countries at this entry point should resist the pressure to deploy complex capabilities before the 
79 
foundations are stable. 
80 
 
81 
Entry point 2 — Integrating existing systems.  
82 
Countries with significant existing investment in programme-specific systems — disease-
83 
specific registries, immunisation systems, maternal and child health tools — face the challenge 
84 
of connecting what they have without replacing it prematurely. The priority here is not 
85 
replacement but integration: establishing the shared infrastructure layer alongside existing 
86 
systems and progressively migrating functions as legacy systems reach end of life or require 
87 
upgrade. For example, a client registry deployed as a shared identification layer, connected to 
88 
existing systems through defined APIs, can immediately reduce duplication without disrupting 
89 
ongoing programme delivery; and an interoperability capability that standardises data exchange 
90 
between existing systems buys time for a more orderly transition to shared components. 
91 
 
92 
Entry point 3 — Upgrading existing infrastructure.  
93 
Countries that have already invested in shared health information infrastructure — but where 
94 
those systems are not built on open standards, are not interoperable with foundational DPI, or 
95 
are controlled by a single supplier — face a different challenge. The priority here is conformance 
96 
and openness; assessing existing systems against open-standards requirements, negotiating 
97 
with suppliers to expose standard APIs, and introducing governance frameworks that prevent 
98 
future lock-in. Countries at this entry point should treat the right to move — the ability to replace, 
99 
upgrade, or migrate to a different system in future — as a non-negotiable condition of any 
100 
technology relationship. 
101 
 
102 
Entry point 4 — Scaling and deepening.  
103 
Countries with established shared infrastructure, strong governance, and advanced digital 
104 
health capability face the challenge of deepening capability rather than establishing it: moving 
105 
from foundational registries toward the business services layer, implementing semantic 
106 
governance infrastructure, extending coverage to the last mile, and demonstrating the value of 
107 
shared infrastructure through measurable health outcomes. The priority here is the sustainability 
108 
of domestically financed national infrastructure, building the enterprise architecture capacity 
109 
within government that can evolve the architecture over time, and engaging Ministries of 
110 
Finance as strategic partners in the long-term financing of shared services.
