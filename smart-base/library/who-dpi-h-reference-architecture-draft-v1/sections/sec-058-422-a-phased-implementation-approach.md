Regardless of entry point, implementation follows a broadly consistent phased progression. The 
114 
phases are not strictly sequential — activities overlap and iterate — but they provide a practical 
115 
structure for planning and sequencing investments, and the activities within each phase vary 
116

with a country's entry point. The indicative timeframes given below describe a typical sequence 
117 
rather than a fixed schedule; actual durations depend heavily on starting conditions, governance 
118 
readiness, and financing, and evaluation continues across all phases rather than only at the 
119 
end. Several threads are not confined to a single phase but run throughout: governance, 
120 
financing, partnership engagement, capacity development, and evaluation begin in Phase 1 and 
121 
continue as the architecture matures. They appear below at the phase where they become most 
122 
prominent, not the phase where they start. 
123 
This phased approach is consistent with, and complements, the WHO Digital Implementation 
124 
Investment Guide (DIIG),35 which sets out a step-by-step, needs-based process for planning 
125 
and costing digital health interventions within a health programme. Where the DIIG guides the 
126 
programme-level investment decision — from health need through costed implementation to 
127 
monitoring — this guidance describes the shared infrastructure those investments align to and 
128 
reuse, so that each programme deployment strengthens the national digital health enterprise 
129 
rather than adding a parallel system. 
130 
Phase 1 — Assess, align, and plan (indicative: the first few months). 
131 
• 
Conduct the national architecture assessment described above, capturing baseline 
132 
values for the outcomes the architecture is expected to influence. 
133 
• 
Establish or strengthen the governance body responsible for digital health architecture 
134 
decisions. 
135 
• 
Engage stakeholders across health programmes, finance, identity, and ICT ministries. 
136 
• 
Select two to three priority health goals, and the specific high-value use cases within 
137 
them that will drive the first implementations and develop the business case and 
138 
evaluation plan to track them. 
139 
• 
Align procurement mechanisms with open-standards and conformance requirements. 
140 
• 
Produce a national digital health architecture roadmap and an initial implementation 
141 
plan, referring to recognised digital-readiness/ capability assessment frameworks. 
142 
• 
Begin the financing and partnership conversation early — engaging Ministries of Finance 
143 
and development partners around the business case, and setting the direction for 
144 
sustainable financing (see section 4.6) 
145 
The investment in this phase is modest but the returns are disproportionately large: countries 
146 
that plan well, position themselves to implement faster and waste less. 
147

148 
months).

151 
task is to assess it against open standards, rationalise duplication, expose standard interfaces,

170 
This phase should produce a functioning, standards-conformant foundation that real health 
171 
programmes are using — not a pilot that would require reconstruction before it can scale.

Phase 3 — Connect and expand (indicative, and strongly context-dependent). 
174 
• 
Progressively connect additional programme systems to the shared foundation. 
175 
• 
Establish semantic governance infrastructure so that terminology and information model 
176 
components remain governed and trustworthy. 
177 
• 
Extend coverage geographically — to subnational levels, to primary care and community 
178 
health, and to offline and low-connectivity environments. 
179 
• 
Begin transitioning functions from legacy systems to shared components as those 
180 
systems reach end of life. 
181 
• 
Develop and deploy the business services layer — clinical decision support, health 
182 
management information and analytics, and public-health surveillance — drawing on the 
183 
foundational shared data layer established earlier. 
184 
Phase 4 — Sustain and evolve (ongoing). 
185 
• 
Transition from externally funded implementation to domestically financed national 
186 
infrastructure. 
187 
• 
Build in-country enterprise architecture capacity within government.
