Building and maintaining the shared digital infrastructure for health is therefore itself central to 
196 
preparing for emerging technologies. The same infrastructure that delivers value across the seven 
197 
priority health goals is what makes AI and other innovations viable, safe, and sustainable. A 
198 
country that has established trusted registries, governed semantics, and reliable interoperability 
199 
is positioned to adopt new technologies as they mature, integrating them into a coherent 
200 
ecosystem rather than attaching them to a fragmented one. The same principle applies to 
201 
genomics, remote monitoring, and decentralised identity alike: the strength of the underlying 
202 
infrastructure determines whether each new technology compounds the value of what already 
203 
exists or simply adds another silo. 
204 
The reference architecture is accordingly designed to remain open and adaptable. Flexibility at 
205 
the interfaces between components, and in how those components are implemented, allows 
206 
emerging technologies — including those not yet foreseeable — to be incorporated and scaled 
207 
as they prove their worth, without the foundations having to be rebuilt. The foundations 
208 
 
 
110 
themselves, however, should not be regarded as static; they require ongoing stewardship, 
209 
maintenance, and incremental evolution to sustain data quality, respond to changing needs, and 
210 
absorb new technologies as they emerge. 
211 
The definition of digital public infrastructure is itself evolving, and it is plausible that capabilities 
212 
now treated as supporting technologies, including aspects of AI, may in time be recognised as 
213 
reusable shared components in their own right. For the present, the architecture treats AI as a 
214 
cross-cutting consideration rather than a defined component or a single platform, reflecting both 
215 
how quickly the field is moving and the fact that analytical needs differ across health goals. The 
216 
architecture remains open to revisiting that position as the technology and the surrounding 
217 
consensus settle. 
218 
Countries are therefore encouraged to view AI and other emerging technologies not as an 
219 
alternative to the foundational investments described in this guidance, but as a compelling 
220 
reason to make and sustain them. Strong digital and data foundations are what allow health 
221 
systems to meet current needs while adopting new technologies safely, effectively, and at scale, 
222 
both now and as the field continues to change.
