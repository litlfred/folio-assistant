TERMINOLOGY 
AND 
ARCHITECTURE 
PLACEMENT 
The Lifelong Health Record (LHR) is the term used in this reference architecture in 
preference to the Shared Health Record (SHR) — the term used in the OpenHIE 
architecture specification — to reflect the longitudinal, person-centred nature of the 
capability: a record that spans a person's entire life and all their interactions with the 
health system, regardless of where or how it is implemented. In the DPI-H reference 
architecture, the LHR sits in the foundational shared data layer alongside the core 
metadata registries for health, the Terminology Service, and the LIMR. Together 
with the core registries, a health record constitutes an essential starting point for a 
health system to deliver digitally-enabled care. The LHR is the governed, person-
centred DPI-H component that aggregates clinical and health-related data across 
those systems and makes it longitudinally available.  
 
5 
1  WHAT IT IS 
 
6 
The Lifelong Health Record (LHR) is one or more governed data repositories, whether physically 
7 
consolidated or virtually federated, that collectively maintain a longitudinal, person-centred record of an 
8 
individual's health and healthcare-related data in computable, standards-based format. It aggregates 
9 
clinical data contributed from multiple source systems — including facility-based care, community health 
10 
programmes, laboratories, pharmacies, and, where applicable, individuals themselves — into a coherent 
11 
view that no single source system produces independently. It makes that view securely accessible to 
12 
authorised health workers, systems, and individuals in accordance with the person's consent preferences 
13 
and the applicable legal framework. 
14 
The LHR is defined by its outcome, not by its implementation model. A centralised national health record, 
15 
a federated architecture in which data remains at source and is assembled on demand, a hybrid approach, 
16 
or a virtual record assembled through standardised query interfaces are all valid expressions of the LHR 
17 
capability. What matters is that the outcome is achieved: a person's clinically relevant health information is 
18 
governed, longitudinal, accessible to authorised actors when and where it is needed, and under the 
19 
meaningful control of the individual to whom it belongs. As a foundational shared data component, the LHR 
20 
is not an optional enrichment to the architecture — it is part of the minimum viable foundation. Without it, 
21 
the continuity of care, clinical decision support, and person-centred capabilities that the DPI-H architecture 
22 
is designed to enable cannot be delivered. The reference architecture defines the outcomes the LHR should 
23 
achieve and the requirements any compliant implementation needs to satisfy, without constraining the 
24 
architectural choices through which countries achieve them. 
25 
The LHR is not a massive central data lake of every clinical fact ever generated by every system. It is a 
26 
curated longitudinal view of a person's clinically relevant data — sufficient to support continuity of care 
27 
across providers, but not intended to duplicate every source system's operational workload. Source 
28 
systems retain their operational data; the LHR holds the governed, shared, person-attributed view that 
29 
crosses institutional and system boundaries. This distinction has significant implications for what countries 
30 
need to build, how data flows are governed, and what consuming applications can reasonably expect to 
31 
retrieve. 
32 
The terms Electronic Medical Record (EMR), Electronic Health Record (EHR), and Shared Health 
33 
Record (SHR) are used across ISO standards, national regulations, and country implementations — 
34 
sometimes interchangeably, sometimes with distinct meanings, and not always consistently with how they 
35 
 
 
156 
are used in this reference architecture. Some clarification of how these terms relate to the LHR is therefore 
36 
necessary.  
37 
The EMR is defined as an electronic record of health-related information that can be created, gathered, 
38 
managed, and consulted by authorised clinicians and staff within one health care organisation56. It is a 
39 
point-of-care tool — the system used by clinicians within a single facility or organisation to manage patient 
40 
encounters.  
41 
The EHR is described variously as an electronic version of a patient's medical history maintained by the 
42 
provider over time57, and ISO 18308:2011 characterises an EHR architecture as one designed to support 
43 
lifelong, effective, high-quality and safe integrated health care through repositories of health information 
44 
that are accessible by multiple authorised users across care settings. Some definitions of the EHR therefore 
45 
approach the scope of what this reference architecture calls the LHR, while others describe what is 
46 
functionally a point-of-care application.  
47 
In the DPI-H reference architecture, these point-of-care clinical applications — variously termed EMRs or 
48 
EHRs across different country contexts — are functional applications that generate and manage clinical 
49 
data at the point of service. They sit at the functional application layer, not the DPI-H layer. The LHR, by 
50 
contrast, is the governed, person-centred foundational component that aggregates data across those 
51 
systems and makes it longitudinally available. Countries that use EHR to refer to this longitudinal, cross-
52 
provider capability should read LHR and EHR as functionally equivalent in this context. 
53 
The LHR is also distinct from the Personal Health Record application through which individuals access their 
54 
data. The PHR application — of which a Digital Health Wallet is a prominent example — is a person-facing 
55 
access layer that presents data from the LHR to the individual. The LHR provides the data foundation; the 
56 
PHR application provides the interface. Neither replaces the other, and the PHR application depends on 
57 
the LHR being in place.

2  WHY IT MATTERS FOR HEALTH OUTCOMES 
 
60 
The dominant pattern of health system digitalization has produced a landscape in which a person's health 
61 
information is distributed across the systems that generated it: a facility's electronic medical record, a 
62 
disease-specific programme registry, a pharmacy's dispensing platform, and a laboratory information 
63 
system each holding a fragment of a clinical picture that is not available as a consolidated view at the point 
64 
of care. When a patient presents at a new facility, their history would need to be reconstructed from what 
65 
they can recall or whatever records they have brought with them, and in many cases, a referral is made 
66 
with the receiving provider having no access to the clinical context that prompted it. For conditions managed 
67 
across multiple providers over many years, no single record connects the encounters — leaving the health 
68 
system to repeatedly re-examine what it already knows, while the burden of continuity falls on the patient 
69 
rather than the infrastructure 
70 
This fragmentation is structural and without a shared, governed capability for maintaining a longitudinal 
71 
person-centred record, every programme and facility that needs health information about an individual 
72 
would need to either maintain its own copy, request it anew at each encounter, or proceed without it. The 
73 
costs compound: duplicated assessments and investigations, avoidable adverse events, missed 
74 
diagnoses, inefficient referrals, and a chronic inability to understand individual health trajectories at the 
75 
population level. 
76 
When a Lifelong Health Record is in place and functioning: 
77 
• 
It enables continuity of care so that a person's clinical history, diagnoses, medications, allergies, 
78 
and care plans are accessible to authorised providers at the point of care regardless of where 
79 
previous encounters took place.

It supports informed clinical decision-making by providing the complete person-level context that

It enables care coordination across providers and programmes, ensuring that transitions of care do

89 
These outcomes are aspirational and require both technical and governance conditions that not all countries 
90 
have yet established. The architecture acknowledges this explicitly. Countries at earlier stages of 
91 
digitalization can pursue LHR capabilities incrementally — starting with shared identity infrastructure and 
92 
exchange standards, progressively connecting programme-specific systems, and extending coverage and 
93 
depth over time. The LHR is not an all-or-nothing proposition; it is a direction of travel, and the reference 
94 
architecture is intended to guide that journey from wherever a country currently stands.

3  CORE ATTRIBUTES 
 
97 
– Longitudinal record aggregation and maintenance. Collection, integration, and ongoing 
98 
maintenance of health data contributed from multiple sources across a person's lifetime — 
99 
including facilities, community health programmes, laboratories, pharmacies, and individuals 
100 
themselves — assembled into a coherent, person-centred record regardless of where data 
101 
originated or which system captured it. 
102 
– Person identity linkage. Association of all health data with a verified individual identity using the 
103 
unique identifier maintained in the Client Registry, ensuring that records from disparate sources 
104 
and encounters across time are correctly and consistently attributed to the right person. The LHR 
105 
does not maintain a parallel or independent person identification capability. 
106 
– Standards-based data storage and semantic consistency. Storage of health data in 
107 
accordance with the logical information structures published through the LIMR and the coded 
108 
terminologies governed by the Terminology Service, ensuring that data contributed from 
109 
heterogeneous source systems is held in a semantically consistent and interoperable form. 
110 
– Consent-governed access control. Enforcement of the individual's expressed consent 
111 
preferences regarding who may access their health data, for what purposes, and under what 
112 
conditions, integrated with consent management services to ensure that access is lawful, 
113 
purposeful, and traceable at all times. 
114 
– Secure, authorised retrieval. Provision of standardised query interfaces through which 
115 
authorised point-of-care systems, analytics platforms, the Computable Decision Support Engine, 
116 
and individuals themselves can retrieve relevant portions of the record based on clinical context, 
117 
encounter type, data category, or time range. 
118 
– Data provenance and auditability. Recording of the source, authoring actor, contributing system, 
119 
and timestamp for every data element held in the record, supporting clinical confidence in the data, 
120 
accountability for its use, and the ability to trace the origin of any clinical finding or decision. 
121 
– Clinical document and discrete data support. Storage and retrieval of both structured clinical 
122 
documents — discharge summaries, referral notes, laboratory reports, immunisation records — 
123 
and discrete coded data elements, accommodating the varying levels of data granularity that exist 
124 
across contributing systems and care settings. 
125 
– Data quality and reconciliation. Identification and management of duplicate, conflicting, or 
126 
incomplete records through reconciliation workflows, ensuring that the longitudinal record remains 
127 
clinically trustworthy over time and across contributing sources. 
128 
 
 
158 
– Individual access and data portability. Provision of mechanisms for individuals to view their own 
129 
health record and to request a portable, machine-readable export in a standardised format, giving 
130 
concrete expression to the individual's right of access and data portability. 
131 
– Availability and resilience. Design for high availability and continuity of access, ensuring that 
132 
authorised users can retrieve critical health information at the point of care even under conditions 
133 
of intermittent connectivity or partial system failure, including support for offline operation and 
134 
synchronisation where required.

4  HOW IT CONNECTS 
 
137 
The Lifelong Health Record depends on foundational components for its integrity, serves as the primary 
138 
data source for a range of consuming systems, and is architecturally distinct from both the point-of-care 
139 
functional applications that generate its data and the person-facing access tools through which individuals 
140 
interact with it. 
141 
The Client Registry is the LHR's most critical dependency. It provides the unique person identifier that the 
142 
LHR uses to link all health data to a verified individual. Without this linkage, records contributed from 
143 
different facilities, programmes, and time periods cannot be reliably associated with the same person. The 
144 
LHR should not maintain a parallel person identification capability; the Client Registry or other foundational 
145 
identity component should be the authoritative identity reference, and person identity within the LHR should 
146 
be derived from it. 
147 
The Health Facility Registry and Health Workforce Registry provide authoritative references for the facilities 
148 
at which health encounters occur and the health workers who author clinical records. The LHR uses these 
149 
identifiers to record encounter provenance, support care coordination, and maintain a complete and 
150 
accountable attribution trail. Point-of-care functional applications — electronic medical records, laboratory 
151 
information systems, pharmacy dispensing platforms — are the primary contributors to the LHR. They are 
152 
not DPI-H components; they are operational applications that sit at the functional application layer and 
153 
connect to the LHR through interoperability infrastructure. 
154 
The Logical Information Model Repository (LIMR) defines the logical structures against which the LHR 
155 
validates and stores health data contributed from source systems. The Terminology Service ensures that 
156 
codes recorded by contributing systems carry consistent meaning within the LHR. Together, these two 
157 
components of the semantic governance infrastructure provide the structural and semantic consistency that 
158 
makes LHR data clinically meaningful and interoperable across the ecosystem. Changes to data models or 
159 
terminology standards must be propagated to the LHR's storage and validation functions, and this 
160 
dependency must be managed through coordinated governance rather than unilateral update. 
161 
The Computable Decision Support Engine (CDSE) consumes person-level clinical data from the LHR to 
162 
evaluate decision logic, generate recommendations, and surface alerts at the point of care. The CDSE does 
163 
not maintain its own copy of clinical data; it queries the LHR in context and at the point of invocation. The 
164 
quality and completeness of the LHR directly determines the reliability of the outputs the CDSE can 
165 
produce. This dependency means that clinical decision support cannot be meaningfully improved in 
166 
isolation from the data quality and coverage of the LHR. 
167 
The Health Management Information System draws on data held in the LHR as a primary source for 
168 
aggregate indicators and programme performance monitoring, deriving population-level health information 
169 
from person-level clinical data rather than requiring separate reporting from health facilities. The Public 
170 
Health Surveillance Platform draws on LHR data for notifiable disease reporting, case investigation, and 
171 
epidemiological analysis, enabling surveillance functions to be grounded in clinical data already captured 
172 
at the point of care. Both relationships reduce the need for parallel data collection and support the 
173 
architecture's principle of capturing data once and using it many times. The LHR provides person-level 
174 
clinical data that these systems aggregate and, where appropriate, de-identify for secondary use.  
175 
For individuals, the primary means of accessing their LHR data is through a Personal Health Record 
176 
application — a functional application that may take the form of a patient portal, a mobile application, or a 
177 
 
 
159 
Digital Health Wallet. These applications depend on the LHR being governed and populated; they do not 
178 
replace or duplicate it.

5  FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS 
 
181 
These requirements are consistent with and extend the OpenHIE Shared Health Record specification 
182 
(SHRWF-1, SHRWF-2; SHRF-1 to SHRF-9). The DPI-H reference architecture adopts 'Lifelong Health 
183 
Record' in preference to 'Shared Health Record' to reflect the longitudinal, federated, and person-centred 
184 
scope of the capability. The DPI-H requirements extend OpenHIE in the following respects: consent 
185 
management integration is made an explicit requirement; federated and distributed architecture support is 
186 
introduced; individual access and portability is added; offline and low-connectivity operation is specified; 
187 
and data quality and reconciliation is made explicit. Where OpenHIE marks requirements as 
188 
Recommended, the DPI-H reference architecture has elevated several to Required given their centrality to 
189 
the clinical safety and governance objectives of the LHR. All OpenHIE SHR requirements that apply are 
190 
noted.

GOVERNANCE 
Governance of the Lifelong Health Record follows the cross-cutting governance 
framework in Section 3.4 of the guidance document. Component-specific 
considerations include:  
(1) Institutional ownership and accountability: Stewardship of the LHR must be vested 
in a health authority with a clear legal mandate for health data governance — not 
delegated to a vendor, donor programme, or vertical health initiative. The LHR serves 
the health system as a whole and must be governed in the public interest.  
(2) Legal and regulatory framework: The LHR must operate within a clearly defined 
legal framework governing collection, storage, access, use, and retention of personal 
 
 
163 
health data. Where such a framework does not yet exist, its development should be 
treated as a prerequisite for deploying the LHR at national scale.  
(3) Consent governance: Countries should define the consent model the LHR will 
operationalise — whether opt-in or opt-out, how granular consent can be, and how 
individuals exercise and revoke it. The consent model must be genuinely 
understandable to individuals and technically enforceable.  
(4) Access policy and role-based governance: The LHR should have a clearly defined 
and publicly documented access policy specifying who may access which categories 
of health data, under what conditions, and for what purposes. Access rights should be 
governed by the individual's consent preferences, the professional role and 
credentials of the requesting actor as verified through the Health Workforce Registry, 
the clinical or operational purpose of the request, and the applicable legal framework. 
Emergency access, break-glass mechanisms, and research access pathways each 
require explicit governance definition. 
(5) Data quality governance: Countries must establish minimum data quality 
requirements before the LHR is declared operational, with clear accountability for 
ongoing data quality management across contributing systems.  
(6) Federated governance. Where the LHR is federated, governance must address 
minimum conformance requirements for distributed nodes, audit and accountability 
mechanisms across the federation, and consistent consent enforcement regardless 
of where data physically resides.  
(7) Data retention and lifecycle management: Countries should define explicit 
retention policies specifying how long different categories of health data are held, what 
happens to records following a person's death, and how clinical utility, legal retention 
requirements, and data protection principles are reconciled. The process for 
correcting or removing erroneous records, and the exercise of data subject rights, 
should also be governed explicitly. 
(8) Openness and transparency. The governance framework, access policies, data 
models, and technical specifications of the LHR should be publicly documented. 
Transparency is a prerequisite for the trust that shared health infrastructure at this 
scale requires. 
(9) Cross-programme and cross-sector governance: The LHR serves multiple 
programmes simultaneously and may interface with actors beyond the health sector. 
Governance should define how data contributed by one programme may be accessed 
by another, what authorisation is required for cross-programme use, and how 
competing institutional interests are resolved in favour of the person-centred purpose 
the LHR is designed to serve.
