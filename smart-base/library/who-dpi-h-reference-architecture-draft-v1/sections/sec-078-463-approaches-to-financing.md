4.6.4 Partnerships 
601 
Partnerships extend well beyond financing. Sustainable DPI-H rests on relationships across 
602 
government — with the authorities responsible for identity, payments, public financial 
603 
management, and interoperability governance, many of which sit outside the health sector — 
604 
and with regional bodies, standards organisations, and the wider implementer community. 
605 
Development partners have a particular responsibility to align their contributions with the 
606 
national architecture and roadmap rather than building parallel systems, and to express support 
607 
in terms of the components and capabilities the country has prioritised. The national digital 
608 
architect and the governance arrangements described earlier in this chapter are the means 
609 
through which these relationships are coordinated in service of a coherent national architecture.

5. 
Future proofing the Architecture 
1 
 
2 
Artificial intelligence and other emerging technologies are reshaping expectations of what digital 
3 
health systems can achieve. Machine learning is being applied to diagnostic and decision support, 
4 
risk prediction, image interpretation, the detection and forecasting of outbreaks, operational 
5 
planning, and resource allocation. Large language and multi-modal models are changing how 
6 
clinical knowledge is summarised, retrieved, and acted upon. Beyond these current applications, 
7 
advances in AI agents, remote monitoring, wearable sensors, genomics, edge computing, and 
8 
verifiable digital credentials each carry implications for how health data is generated, exchanged, 
9 
and used. The pace of this change is unlikely to slow. 
10 
This guidance does not attempt to catalogue emerging technologies or to predict their trajectories, 
11 
both of which would date quickly. Its purpose is to set out the relationship between emerging 
12 
technologies and the shared digital infrastructure described in this document, and to offer practical 
13 
orientation for countries weighing where and how to adopt them. For artificial intelligence in 
14 
particular, that orientation matters: implementers consistently report that the difficulty is less 
15 
whether to adopt AI than knowing what, specifically, to adopt and on what foundations. General 
16 
encouragement to adopt AI offers little to a ministry deciding where to invest. 
17 
Artificial intelligence is understood here broadly, as machine-based systems that, for a given set 
18 
of objectives, infer from the data they receive how to generate outputs such as predictions, 
19 
recommendations, classifications, or decisions. This spans rule-based systems, statistical and 
20 
machine learning models, and the large language and multi-modal models whose rapid public 
21 
adoption from the early 2020s brought AI to the centre of health-sector attention. Several 
22 
jurisdictions are now establishing legal definitions and governance frameworks for these 
23 
systems42, and WHO has issued guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health43 and, 
24 
more recently, on large multi-modal models specifically44.
