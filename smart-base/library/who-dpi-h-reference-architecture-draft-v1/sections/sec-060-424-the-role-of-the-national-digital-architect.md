A recurring finding from country experience and global dialogue is that implementation fails not 
228 
for lack of technology but for lack of someone accountable for the architecture as a whole. The 
229 
 
 
94 
Wilton Park dialogue on digital public infrastructure for health36 identified the national digital 
230 
architect — an individual or small team embedded in government, with the authority to define 
231 
architectural standards, evaluate conformance, and enforce coordination across programmes 
232 
and ministries — as one of the most urgent and actionable priorities for countries pursuing DPI-
233 
H. This role is not an IT manager, a project coordinator, or a donor-funded consultant. It is the 
234 
accountable designer of the health system’s digital infrastructure — the person who holds the 
235 
architectural blueprint, ensures every investment conforms to it, and has the authority to say no, 
236 
or to escalate, when a proposed investment would undermine it. 
237 
Because architectural coherence has to be maintained well beyond any single project, this 
238 
should be established as an enduring function — part of business-as-usual governance — 
239 
rather than a time-limited role, and put in place before major implementation begins, not after 
240 
fragmentation has already set in, whether within the Ministry of Health, a dedicated digital health 
241 
agency, or a whole-of-government digital authority. Countries need not face this in isolation: 
242 
regional and global networks of national architects provide a means to share plans, compare 
243 
approaches, and learn from one another’s experience. Progress along the path described here 
244 
can then be tracked using the conformance and progress measures set out in section 4.4.
