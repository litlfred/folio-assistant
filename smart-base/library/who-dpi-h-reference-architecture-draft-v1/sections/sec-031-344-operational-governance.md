Operational governance manages the day-to-day integrity and performance of the shared 
335 
infrastructure within the mandate established by strategic governance. It is accountable for 
336 
ensuring that the infrastructure operates reliably, that data quality is maintained, that changes are 
337 
managed safely, and that technical and operational decisions are made transparently and 
338 
consistently. Its responsibilities include: 
339 
• Master data management — maintaining the quality, currency and authority of the core 
340 
registries (client, health facility, health workforce, product, terminology) through defined 
341 
stewardship roles and processes. Responsibility for this data is frequently distributed rather 
342 
than held by a single body: different authorities are the custodians of different parts of the 
343 
data — a medicines regulatory authority for product master data, for example — and a clear, 
344 
documented assignment of who owns and stewards which data helps avoid both governance 
345 
paralysis and the fragmentation that arises when several bodies maintain their own versions 
346 
of the same data. 
347 
• Release management — governing the introduction of changes to the infrastructure (new 
348 
versions, configurations, integrations and extensions) through structured review, testing and 
349 
deployment. 
350 
• Change management — coordinating the implementation of changes across stakeholder 
351 
communities with appropriate communication, documentation and rollback provisions. 
352 
 
 
57 
• Service levels and incident management — defining and monitoring availability, response 
353 
and recovery standards, and managing incidents and their resolution. 
354 
• Interoperability and semantic governance — maintaining and enforcing conformance with 
355 
the infrastructure's technical and semantic standards, including the registration and lifecycle 
356 
management of terminologies, value sets and information models through the Semantic 
357 
Governance Infrastructure described in 3.4.5. 
358 
Operational governance is also where the evaluation of systems in use is exercised in practice: 
359 
monitoring is not only of availability but of how the infrastructure is actually used and where 
360 
unintended consequences emerge, feeding what is learned back into both operational adjustment 
361 
and strategic review. 
362 
Escalation pathways. Operational governance works within a defined mandate. When issues 
363 
arise that cross scope boundaries, carry equity or sustainability implications, or exceed 
364 
operational authority, structured escalation to strategic governance is essential. Escalation 
365 
triggers should be defined explicitly — for example, requested changes that significantly expand 
366 
or contract the infrastructure's scope or user base; utilisation that approaches the thresholds for 
367 
resource-overuse response; conflicts between stakeholder needs that cannot be resolved within 
368 
operational authority; security incidents or data breaches with potential systemic consequences; 
369 
and funding or resource constraints that threaten continuity. Escalation should be documented, 
370 
time-bound, and resolved with a clear decision communicated back to the operational body, so 
371 
that escalated issues are not left unresolved.
