3.4.3 Strategic governance 
290 
Strategic governance provides the authoritative direction for the shared infrastructure. It defines 
291 
what the infrastructure exists to achieve, who it serves, what is within and outside its scope, and 
292 
how resources are secured and allocated to sustain it. It does not manage operations; it creates 
293 
the mandate within which operations function. Its responsibilities include: 
294 
• 
establishing and maintaining the infrastructure's mission, scope and core values; 
295 
• 
safeguarding financial and institutional sustainability across funding cycles;  
296 
• 
approving major architectural or strategic changes that cross organisational boundaries 
297 
or affect multiple stakeholders;  
298 
• 
ensuring that underserved and underrepresented communities have genuine voice in its 
299 
direction;  
300 
• 
monitoring for systemic risks such as resource overuse, inequitable access and 
301 
dependency concentration; and  
302 
• 
defining the escalation triggers and response protocols for issues that exceed the 
303 
operational mandate. 
304 
 
305 
Safeguarding the shared resource 
306 
Three of the principles in 3.4.2 are substantive rather than procedural: they concern what the 
307 
shared infrastructure should remain — adequately funded, equitable in whom it serves, and 
308 
protected from overuse. These are the ways shared public infrastructure most characteristically 
309 
fails, and stating them as principles does not secure them; a principle does not fund the 
310 
infrastructure, give underserved groups a place in governance, or cap anyone's usage.

Securing financial continuity. Strategic bodies should define a multi-stakeholder funding model 
313 
that identifies who benefits from the infrastructure and in what ways, and what contribution is 
314 
appropriate for each category of beneficiary; establish reserve and contingency mechanisms that 
315 
maintain continuity through funding gaps and transitions; monitor utilisation against capacity and 
316 
hold the authority to adjust access, contribution requirements or fee structures to preserve the 
317 
integrity of the infrastructure; and engage proactively with national health financing mechanisms, 
318 
development partners and multilateral institutions so that DPI-H is positioned within sustainable 
319 
funding frameworks rather than project-based cycles. 
320 
Operationalising equity in governance bodies. Design considerations include formal 
321 
representation of civil society and community health advocates with a demonstrated constituency 
322 
in underserved populations; geographic representation across subnational regions, particularly 
323 
where health system capacity varies; gender and linguistic inclusion in the composition of 
324 
governance bodies; mechanisms for soliciting and incorporating feedback from communities that 
325 
lack organised representation; and explicit criteria by which equity impacts are assessed when 
326 
major decisions are made. 
327 
Managing demand on shared capacity. Strategic governance should establish usage tiers or 
328 
policies calibrated to an actor's capacity and benefit; monitoring and reporting that makes 
329 
utilisation patterns visible to governance bodies on a regular basis; defined response protocols 
330 
ranging from engagement and negotiation to access restrictions; contribution requirements — 
331 
financial, technical or in-kind — scaled to the benefit derived; and periodic review of whether the 
332 
distribution of costs and benefits remains equitable and sustainable.
