Legacy systems are not the enemy of DPI-H — unplanned legacy systems are. Countries that 
199 
approach legacy systems with a clear framework can make principled decisions about what to 
200 
preserve, what to integrate, and what to retire. 
201 
 
202 
Assess before you act. Do not assume a legacy system should be replaced because it 
203 
predates the reference architecture. Assess whether it performs a function aligned with 
204 
REDDHI, whether it can be made conformant through API exposure or standards adoption, and 
205 
whether replacement would deliver sufficient value to justify the disruption. 
206 
 
207 
Integrate before you replace. Where a legacy system holds data that the health system 
208 
depends on and could not easily recreate, integration is almost always preferable to 
209 
replacement in the near term. Expose the system’s data through standard APIs, connect it to an 
210 
interoperability capability where that is the most viable approach, and plan for an orderly 
211 
transition rather than a disruptive replacement. 
212 
 
213 
Replace at natural transition points. Procurement cycles, system end-of-life events, and 
214 
major upgrade decisions are natural moments to shift from a legacy system to shared, 
215 
interoperable infrastructure aligned with REDDHI. Countries should plan for these moments in 
216 
advance, ensuring that replacement systems are selected and procured against open-standards 
217 
requirements and that the data held in legacy systems is migrated into the shared foundation. 
218 
 
219 
Stop creating new silos. The most important principle for managing legacy systems is to stop 
220 
adding to them. Every new system procured without conformance to open standards and 
221 
integration with shared infrastructure adds to the legacy debt the country eventually has to 
222 
address. Countries should treat architectural conformance as a mandatory procurement 
223 
requirement, not an optional enhancement.
