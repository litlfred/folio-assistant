Cost and sustainability are practical constraints rather than afterthoughts. Models differ markedly 
178 
in the computing resources they require, pricing for commercial offerings can be volatile, and 
179 
dependence on a single provider carries a risk of lock-in. Architectural choices that preserve the 
180 
ability to substitute one model for another, and that separate the orchestration of a service from 
181 
any particular model, reduce this exposure and keep options open as the technology and its 
182 
economics evolve. Some countries are, in addition, exploring sovereign models — models hosted 
183 
and governed within national or regional control — to keep sensitive data and critical capabilities 
184 
under domestic authority; this can support data sovereignty and reduce dependence on a single 
185 
external provider, though it carries its own cost and capacity implications. 
186 
It is also worth being explicit about the commercial, economic, and political pressures that 
187 
accompany emerging technologies. The visibility of AI can generate pressure — from vendors, 
188 
donors, and political expectation — to invest in advanced capabilities before the foundations that 
189 
make them viable are in place. A country that builds AI on fragmented foundations does not 
190 
leapfrog the foundational work; it defers it at a higher eventual cost, and risks isolated pilots that 
191 
perform well in controlled conditions but prove difficult to scale, generalise, or govern in routine 
192 
practice. A more considered sequence — foundations first, capabilities as their value is 
193 
demonstrated — tends to serve both better.
