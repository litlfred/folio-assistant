Numbers

In general, words should be used for the numbers one to nine and at the
beginning of sentences, and figures for 10 and above; however, editors
should use their judgement in individual cases. Figures should never-
theless always be used in specific numerical contexts (e.g. “day 3” or “a
factor of 2”). Numbers that constitute a series or range should usually
be written as figures if at least one of them is greater than nine, for
example “we treated 19 patients with drug A and 7 with drug B”.

When anumber has more than four digits, it should be split by means
of a thin space (not a comma) after every three digits to the left or right
of the decimal point (e.g. 10 000, 0.234 56), but in a table column
containing a mixture of numerals with four digits and numerals with
more than four digits, align them by spacing all of them as for the latter

group.

A decimal point should always be preceded by a figure, if necessary a
zero. Very small numbers are usually best given in exponential nota-
tion, for example 1.6789 x 10°.

The term “billion” is ambiguous (meaning 10° in American and often
10" in British usage) and should be defined or avoided.

When a colon is used to indicate a ratio, there should be a thin space
on either side of it.

Dates
Dates should be written in the form 12 September 1989.

Time

For the time of day, the 24-hour clock should generally be used, e.g.
16:30.

Units of measurement

Measurements should normally be given in SI units in accordance with
The SI for the health professions; for summary tables, see section 11. Most
SI symbols are lower-case letters except those derived from proper
names (e.g. Pa, Gy, W). Symbols are identical in the singular and

Figures should always be used with unit symbols and abbreviations, for
example 7 kg not seven kg; the figure should be separated from the unit
symbol by a thin space. To avoid ambiguity, no more than one solidus
should be used to divide units, for example m/s? (or m-s~?) not m/s/s.
When a medial multiplication point is used, it should be clearly
identified.

Exceptionally, for blood pressure, values may still be given in millimetres
of mercury with the equivalent in kilopascals in parentheses, for
example

120 mmHg (16 kPa),

and in texts on nutrition, values for energy may be quoted in both
joules and thermochemical calories, for example

5.69 MJ (1360 kcal,,.).

Concentration

The term “concentration” has been applied to numerous different
quantities. The appropriate units for measuring three of the most
common are: substance concentration, mol/m? (or mol/l); mass con-
centration, kg/m? (or kg/l); and mass fraction, kg/kg. Wherever pos-
sible, the first of these should be used. The symbol M should not be
substituted for mol/l, and the term “molarity” should not be used.

Relative molecular mass

The relative molecular mass of a substance (M,) is the ratio of the mass
of one molecule of that substance to one-twelfth of the mass of an atom
of carbon-12. It is therefore a pure number and dimensionless. The
term “molecular weight” should no longer be used.

Temperature
Temperature should be given in the form 7 °C or 20-25 °C. The degree
sign is part of the unit and should be closed up to the C.

13. Mathematics and statistics

Mathematical matter and equations

Italics should be used for mathematical variables and certain physical
constants, roman type for operators (such as +, =, d and multipli-
cation and integral signs), abbreviations (such as log, sin and exp) and
representations of pure numbers (such as e and i).

Badly written or ambiguous symbols and signs should be identified for
the typesetter (e.g. italic letter “x”/Greek chi/multiplication sign;
capital letter “O”/Greek theta/zero), and the spatial relationship of
terms made clear. When several brackets have to be used in a math-
ematical expression, the sequence should be {[ (...) ] }.

Mathematical formulae should be written in such a way as to take up
as little space as possible, for example

x/(a—b) not __x ,

(a—b)

except when this could cause difficulty in understanding or lead to
confusion.

Mark thin spaces before and after mathematical operators, except
when + or—is used to indicate a positive or negative number (e.g. —6)
and around a medial multiplication point or solidus.

Equations for display should be clearly marked and guidance provided
on where to break those that are likely to be too long to fit on one line
(preferably before an operational sign and not in the middle of a term).
The second line may be set flush right, a standard indention from the
right margin, or aligned on operational signs. Equations (whether in
line or displayed) that form part of sentences may need punctuation
depending on their context.

In statistical analyses, the mean should be supported by statistics such
as the number of observations, the standard deviation (a measure of the
variability of a sét of data) or the standard error (a measure of the

variability in the form SE = +1.3, which wrongly implies that SE can be
negative. Probability values are usually quoted in the form “P < 0.001”.
