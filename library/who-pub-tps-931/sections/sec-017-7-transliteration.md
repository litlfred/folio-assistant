It is usual to transliterate Arabic and Russian from their original
alphabets to the Latin alphabet, and to transcribe Chinese into Latin
characters, ie. to romanize the language. Editors should consult the
Office of Language Services (TRA) if they are in any doubt about the
transliteration of particular words or phrases.

Arabic

The system of transliteration used by the United Nations is given in
document ES/TRANS/84/3/rev 2, Capitalization and spelling of some
terms related to the Middle East and North Africa. This document also
contains useful information on the official names of various people,
places and organizations often mentioned in connection with Arab
affairs. A copy is kept in CTT. References in general texts to well
known people or places should employ the spelling familiar to English-
speaking readers, e.g. Mecca not Makka.

Note the following in relation to Arabic proper names:

© The word “al” often represents the definite article, in which case
it is attached to the following word with a hyphen, and is lower
case, e.g. Mr Hafez al-Assad.

e Abd (al-), “Servant (of the)” cannot be separated from the name
that follows. A reference, for instance, to Mr Abd al-Qadir can-
not subsequently be shortened to Mr Al-Qadir or Mr Qadir.

Russian

There are a number of systems for transliterating Russian. The ISO
“tinguistic system”, making free use of diacritical marks and with a one-
to-one correspondence between letters, is recommended for use in
WHO publications. The most recent version is ISO 9-1995: Informa-
tion and documentation — Transliteration of Cyrillic characters into
Latin characters — Slavic and non-Slavic languages. A slightly differ-
ent older version (ISO/R9-1954) is currently in general use in WHO.

Regardless of the system used, well known Russian names should be
given in the form familiar to English-speaking readers, e.g. Moscow,
Tchaikovsky. Similarly, if an author or a participant in a meeting

Chinese

The romanization system called pinyin, introduced by the Chinese in
the 1950s, has now largely supplanted the older Wade-Giles system.
Pinyin should be used in all WHO publications; accents are not
generally necessary, except in certain specific contexts (e.g. texts on
acupuncture). Pinyin spellings often differ markedly from the older
ones, and personal names are usually spelt without apostrophes. Given
names tend to be closed up rather than hyphenated in pinyin, but
current practice is variable. A list of Chinese place names, giving the
pinyin spelling, the Han character name, and the usual English,
French and Spanish equivalents is available from TPS.

8. Copyright and disclaimers

When a manuscript is accepted for publication by WHO, it becomes
the property of the Organization, the author ceding the copyright. The
following copyright notice and disclaimers are printed on the verso of
the title page of all headquarters publications:

The World Health Organization welcomes requests for permission
to reproduce or translate its publications, in part or in full. Applica-

Reproducing previously published material

The originating unit is responsible for obtaining the permission of the
copyright holders — publisher or author or both — to reproduce any
previously published material. Where necessary, editors should draw
the author’s attention to the need to obtain permission. A standard
letter is available for this (see WHO Manual VIII.2, Annex C). The
originals of the letters granting such permission should be sent to PUB
with the manuscript, if possible, or as soon as received.

Quotations from written material protected under copyright are nor-
mally considered to be “fair use”, provided they are of reasonable
length and the source is mentioned. However, drawings, tables,
photographs, etc. may not be used without permission, even if the copy
has been altered slightly.

Material published in the USA by federal or publicly funded agencies,
e.g. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and National Insti-
tutes of Health, is considered to be in the public domain and not
protected by copyright. The source should, in any case, be acknowl-

edged.

9. Reference lists and bibliographies

A clear distinction should be made between lists of references and
bibliographies. A list of references contains only those works cited in
the text as sources of data or other information. It may include
unpublished documents, provided that these are available to inter-
ested readers. A bibliography is a list of published works relevant to
the subject matter of the publication and recommended for further
reading.

Authors are responsible for ensuring the accuracy of bibliographic
information. However, the editor should check any details that are
obviously wrong or that appear doubtful.

Bibliographies

Bibliographies in WHO publications should be reasonably representa-
tive of the world literature on the subject. It should be remembered
that most WHO books are translated into at least French and Spanish.
The names of authors should be arranged alphabetically, but a bibliog-
raphy may be subdivided into sections by subject. Bibliographies
should not contain references to unpublished works.

References

References may be cited in the text using either a numerical system or
the Harvard system. The numerical system is preferred, and is obliga-
tory for the Technical Report Series, the Bulletin of the World Health
Organization and World health forum. A reference to a finding reported
in an unpublished document should simply be followed, in parenthe-
ses, by the statement “unpublished data” or “unpublished observa-
tions” accompanied, if appropriate, by the name of the authority cited
and the date (P. Butler, unpublished data, 1991). Personal communi-
cations should be dealt with similarly. However, if an unpublished
document is known to be easily available, it may be included in the
reference list, together with an indication of how it may be obtained
(see example on page 29).

Nomerical system
The references are numbered consecutively as they occur in the text,
the number being in italic (underlined in the manuscript) and placed

Harvard system
The references are cited by giving the names of the authors followed

ways:

It has been reported (Ballance, Butler & Ewart, 1989; Allsopp,
1990) that ...

Bibliographic style

Bibliographic style in WHO publications is based on the “Uniform
requirements for manuscripts submitted to biomedical journals” (the
so-called Vancouver style), formulated by the International Commit-
tee of Medical Journal Editors, but with certain adaptations to meet
WHO?’s particular needs. (It should be noted in any case that the
Vancouver style is intended to provide instructions to authors on how
to prepare their manuscripts, and is not a style guide for editors.)
