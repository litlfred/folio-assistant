Parenthesis

A parenthesis can be marked off by commas, dashes or brackets,
depending upon the closeness of its relationship to the sentence.

Parentheses should be used sparingly since they tend to break up
sentence structure and can too often be used to save the trouble of
organizing one’s thoughts.

Spaced em or en rules are used as parenthetical dashes. If unspaced in
the typescript, they should have a vertical line on either side, but a
space sign is unnecessary.

Dashes
Hyphen

It is not possible to be didactic about hyphens without being mislead-
ing, but they should be avoided whenever possible and introduced only
to avoid ambiguity.

The practice of separating two or more prefixes to the same word,
leaving a hyphen in mid-air, should be avoided if possible, e.g.

... hypertension or hypotension ... not ... hyper- or hypotension ...

Use of the hyphen with appropriate examples is described in greater
detail in section 2.

En rule

An en tule is longer than a hyphen and is most commonly used to
convey a distinction in sense when the first part of the compound does
not modify the meaning of the second part — unlike the hyphen. The
en rule in these circumstances can usually be thought of as standing for
“and” or “to”, and is usually unspaced, e.g.

case-control study
benefit-risk ratio

oil-water interface
gas-liquid chromatography.

In the above examples a solidus (oblique stroke) would convey the
same meaning, e.g. oil/water interface.

The en rule signifies “to” in the following:

12-24 September
pp. 5-55
London-Brighton race.

However, if the word “from” is used, e.g. from 1850 to 1895, then the
en tule is inappropriate and “to” must be used.

For typographical reasons, a spaced en rule is sometimes used — as in
this manual — to perform the functions of an em rule.
