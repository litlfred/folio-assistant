]. Introduction

This style manual is intended primarily for use by WHO editors
working in English. Its main purpose is to ensure that the language used
in WHO publications is clear and correct and that the scientific and
other special nomenclature and terminology, abbreviations and sym-
bols are reasonably consistent and follow established international
precedents.

Editors should treat the manual as a work of reference to be consulted
in case of doubt, and not asa standard against which all details of every
manuscript must routinely be matched. Time should not be spent in
the pursuit of uniformity as an end in itself. The manual is intended to
speed up the preparation of manuscripts, not retard it.

Other WHO staff members who write articles or reports for eventual
publication by WHO will greatly facilitate the work of the editors if
they follow the conventions established here. However, the manual is
not intended to be a guide to English grammar or to good writing:
readers seeking such a guide are referred to The elements of style, 3rd ed.
(Strunk W, White EB. New York, Macmillan, 1978) and A guide to
writing for the United Nations (New York, United Nations, 1984).

The manual has been prepared by the Technical Publications sub-
unit, Office of Publications (PUB/TPS). It will be updated as necessary
and revised in the light of experience with its use. Comments on the
content of the manual and suggestions for changes or additions
are welcome and should be sent to PUB/TPS.

2. Spelling

This section is a general guide to spelling used in WHO publications,
and also gives guidelines on hyphenation and capitalization. It is not
intended to be comprehensive, but to cover the main areas, such as
scientific and medical terminology, and names of places and organiza-
tions, where confusion and uncertainty may arise.

Within WHO, British rather than American spelling is normally used.
The general rule is to follow the first spelling listed in the latest edition
of The concise Oxford dictionary. If a variant spelling is listed separately,
the one to which you are referred should be used; e.g. “tire, US var. of
tyre”: use “tyre”.

There are, however, a number of exceptions:

© where adifferent spelling has become established usage in WHO;

© where there is anced to respect the recommendations of interna-
tional nomenclature-setting bodies;

© quoted material, where the original spelling must be reproduced
exactly; and

* book and article titles, where the original spelling must be
reproduced.

Annex 1 gives the preferred spellings of many of the commonly
encountered exceptions; it also includes The concise Oxford dictionary
spellings that should be followed for many words that often cause doubt
or difficulty, and gives some guidelines on usage and choice of words.

For transliteration from Arabic, Russian and Chinese see section 7.

Eponymous names give no information about the nature of the disease
and different names may be used in different countries. They should be
avoided whenever possible. The Saxon genitive (’s) is not necessary
when using an eponymous name; e.g. Crohn disease, not Crohn’s
disease, and Down syndrome, not Down’s syndrome.

For general reference, Butterworths medical dictionary, 2nd ed. (Critchley
M, ed. London, Butterworths, 1978) and Dorland’s illustrated medical
dictionary, 28th ed. (Philadelphia, Saunders, 1994) can be recom-
mended for British and American spelling, respectively.

Progress in medical terminology (Manuila A, ed. Basel, Karger, 1981)
describes the evolution of medical terminology and gives a detailed
account of the development of the IND.

For scientific spelling and terminology see section 14.

Country and place names

Webster’s new geographical dictionary (Springfield, Merriam-Webster,
1984) can be used as 4 guide to the spelling of common geographical
entities. However, country names and their spelling, in particular, can
change at an alarming rate. The Computer-Assisted Translation and
Terminology subunit (CTT), which coordinates with the Office of the
Legal Counsel (LEG), can provide up-to-date information on the
names of United Nations and WHO Member States, including their
full, short and adjectival/attributive forms.

The official names of United Nations Member States and approved
and alternative names for various cities and countries are listed in
Annexes 2 and 3. For countries such as “The Gambia” and “The
Netherlands”, the definite article may be omitted in address lists and
enumerations.

Syllabification

The first edition of Collins dictionary of the English language (Hanks P,
ed. London, Collins, 1979) gives syllabification breaks and appropriate
hyphenation points for each entry. The position of syllabic breaks
where hyphenation is permissible is based upon phonetic rather than
etymological principles, e.g. cata-lyse, but ca-taly-sis; meta-bol-ic, but
met-abo-lism.

Hyphenation

Hyphensare used to connect words that are more closely linked to each
other than to the surrounding syntax. Unfortunately there are no hard
and fast rules about their use. Certain conventions exist, but if there is
a choice it is better to introduce hyphens only to avoid ambiguity.
There is an overall tendency to use hyphens less than in the past and
a few general rules are applicable, as follows.

Hyphen after a prefix

A hyphen should be used after a prefix in order to prevent the word
being mistaken for another one, e.g. co-op, coop (but cooperate needs
no hyphen); re-cover, recover; un-ionized, unionized.

Prefixes such as anti- and semi- can often be used without a hyphen,
e.g. antihypertensive, antidysrhythmic, semiconductor, semicircular.
However, a hyphen is required to avoid doubling a vowel or a
consonant, e.g. anti-inflammatory, anti-infective, semi-invalid.

A hyphen is also required when the prefix is to a word beginning
with a capital letter, e.g. anti-Darwinian, sub-Saharan (but note
Pan American Health Organization).

Hyphens with compound nouns and adjectives

Some compound words remain hyphenated irrespective of their gram-
matical use, e.g. well-being, side-effect, extra-articular, forty-two,
Vice-President, Director-General. Others are hyphenated according
to whether they are used as nouns or adjectives, e.g. first-class (adj.) but
first class (n.), low-cost (adj.) but low cost (n.), or whether they are
used attributively or as predicates, e.g. up-to-date statistics but the
statistics are up to date.

It is usually unnecessary to insert a hyphen after an adverb, e.g.
carefully developed plan, well known fact, buta hyphen is appropriate
when an adverb might be mistaken for an adjective (compare little-
used car and little used car).

Capitalization

Too many capital letters break the flow of the eye across a sentence and
down acolumn of type. However, proper names, titles and institutions
require capitals. Descriptive appellations, such as administrative posts,
do not, unless the result would appear odd or confusing.

The trade names of drugs and other proprietary products, e.g. Pyrex and
Vaseline (petroleum jelly preferred), require an initial capital letter.
Generic terms in the Linnaean classification also take an initial capital
letter, e.g. Staphylococcus aureus, Trypanosoma, Schistosoma, but trypa-
nosome, schistosome, etc.

Article, book and journal titles in references need not be capitalized,
e.g. Social science and medicine, except when they contain the name of
an association or organization, e.g. Bulletin of the World Health Organi-
zation, Journal of the American Medical Association.

Words ending in -ize, -ise and -yse

Where there is a choice between using the suffix -ize or -ise (e.g.
organize or organise), -ize, derived from the Greek “-izo”, is preferred,
consistent with the first spelling of such words given in The concise
Oxford dictionary. For some words, such as surprise, comprise or impro-
vise, where -ise is not a suffix but part of the root of the word, there is
no choice and -ise must be used. Similarly, -yse must be used for words
derived from the Greek lusis (= lysis), as in analyse, dialyse and
hydrolyse.

Alphabetical arrangement

In lists of names and addresses, indexes, alphabetical reference lists,
etc., the following general rules should be followed:

¢ When the first component of a family name is a particle such as
“de”, “Le”, “al-”, “Van” or “von”, the first letter of the particle
should normally be used for alphabetization, unless established
usage or another specific reason dictates otherwise.

© Names beginning with the prefix “Mac” or “Mc” should be

arranged as if the letter “a” were present in all cases.

° When Chinese personal names are written in the traditional way,
the single-syllable family name comes first and should be used for
alphabetizing (e.g. Dr Hu Ching-Li should be listed under “H”).

¢ In the alphabetization of chemical names, Greek letters and
italicized prefixes should be ignored.

3. Punctuation

This is a large subject where personal taste and common sense are more
important than any rules. Appropriate punctuation will ease reading
and clarify meaning, and this should be its purpose.

A well written running text should require only the minimum of
punctuation in order to read clearly and sensibly. If much punctuation
is required, it is probably a good indication that the texthas been badly
written and should be revised rather than heavily punctuated. Particu-
larly in scientific writing, unnecessary punctuation should be avoided
if the meaning is clear.

The punctuation guidelines below do not cover the special require-
ments of references and bibliographies (see section 9), or those of
indexes.

Care should be taken to make sure that, when appropriate, punctua-
tion marks are printed in the same style and type font as the rest of the
text in which they appear.

Very few specific examples of the use of punctuation marks appear in
this section, since they are to be found in abundance in the books listed
on page 15.

Full point (period)

A full point should be used to separate statements between which there
is no true continuity of thought. This results in shorter, more concise
sentences, thus avoiding the temptation to include too much in a
single sentence.

No full point is required for column headings, running heads, chapter
titles and legends, or after abbreviations ending with the final letter of
the word (see “Abbreviations and symbols”, section 11, for examples).

An abbreviating full point, as at the end of etc. and Co., is omitted
before a full point ending a sentence. Similarly, a stronger punctuation
mark, such as a question mark or an exclamation mark, will replace a
full point at the end of a sentence.

Ellipsis points (three spaced dots) signify either omitted material or
faltering speech. Interruptions or abrupt changes in thought should be
indicated by a dash (em rule).

Comma

Correct usage of the comma is often a question of judgement. Commas
are now used much less frequently than in the past, and their main
purpose is to indicate a pause or to avoid ambiguity.

In a list of three or more items, a comma should in general be
consistently inserted or omitted before the final “and”, e.g.

X, Y and Z or X, Y, and Z.

Editors should ensure that each publication is internally consistent in
this respect. However, even when the “X, Y and Z” system is adopted,
an additional comma should be inserted wherever necessary to avoid
ambiguity, e.g.

W, X and Y, and Z differs in meaning from W, X, and Y and Z.

Commas can often be used instead of parentheses and when separating
relative clauses within a sentence.

It is usual to-omit the comma when printing house numbers in
addresses.

Semicolon

The semicolon marks a longer pause or more definite break than the
comma. It links clauses or sentences too closely related to be cut off by
a full point, but not closely enough related to be joined by a conjunce-
tion.

The semicolon is used to separate two or more linked clauses of more
or less equal importance.

In asentence containing long clauses with commas, the semicolon is
used to indicate major breaks or appropriate pauses.

The first word following a semicolon always starts with a lower-case

letter, unless it is a proper noun.
