The scientific terminology used in WHO publications should be in
accordance with the recommendations of the relevant international
bodies. Guidance on correct usage can be obtained from CTT.

Anatomy

In general, the anglicized versions of Latin anatomical terms should be
used, as found in the standard medical dictionaries. If, for a specific
reason, Latin terms are preferred in a given context, they should not be
italicized.

Animals, plonts, bacteria and viruses
The relevant codes of international nomenclature should be used.

The name of a genus is set in italic with an initial capital (e.g.
Staphylococcus); it refers to all species and subspecies within the genus
and should not be used as a substitute for the name of a single species.
The name of a species consists of the genus name and the specific
epithet, both italicized (e.g. Escherichia coli). The name of the person
who originally classified the species sometimes follows the binomen in
roman (e.g. Schistosoma mansoni Sambon). A genus name may be
abbreviated to its initial letter after the first mention, unless this leads
to ambiguity.

Common names (not italicized) also exist for certain genera and
species, for example:

pseudomonad
salmonella
blackfly
gorilla.

Both one-word and two-word common names are in use for viruses
(e.g. poliovirus, papillomavirus, poxvirus, herpesvirus; but influenza
virus, cowpox virus, rubella virus, mumps virus).

chemistry

Chemical names should follow the IUPAC tules, as interpreted by
the American Chemical Society (see Chemical abstracts). Note that
“sulfur” should always be used rather than “sulphur”, but that the Brit-
ish spellings “aluminium” and “caesium” are preferted to “aluminum”
and “cesium”.

General

Chemical names should be spelt out in text unless the symbolic
formula is graphically useful. In one-line formulae used to represent
molecules, dashes may be used to represent bonds in the main chain,
for example:

HN — [CH,], — CH(NH,) — CO.H.

If there is no need to show linkages, the formula may be written
without bond symbols, for example:

(CH,),CHCH,CH(NH,)COOH.

Isotopic labelling

The symbol for the isotope introduced is placed in square brackets
directly attached to the part of the name to which it refers (e.g. sodium
('4C]formate); appropriate arabic numerals and prefixes are added as
necessary (e.g. L-[2-C]leucine). For simple molecules, chemical
formulae may be used (e.g. 4#CO,).

Square brackets should not be used when the isotopic symbol is
attached to a word that is not a chemical name (e.g. “!*C-labelled”).
Terms such as “'![-labelled albumin” should not be contracted to
[3!T]albumin since native albumin does not contain iodine.

In text, an isotope can be identified in the following form:
phosphorus-32 — iodine-131.

Diseases

As far as possible the International Nomenclature of Diseases should
be followed for disease names and their spelling; otherwise standard
dictionaries (e.g. Stedman’s or Dorland’s) should be consulted. It
should be noted that the International Classification of Diseases
(ICD-10) is a statistical classification and is not intended to be a
reference for nomenclature.

Drugs and pesticides

For pharmaceuticals the International Nonproprietary Names (INNs)
should be used, and for pesticides the common names adopted by the
International Organization for Standardization (see International Stan-
dard ISO 1750:1981, and subsequent addenda and amendments). When
no internationally approved name exists, an approved national name
may be used. Proprietary names should be avoided whenever possible.

Genetics

Genes and chromosomes

The International Committee on Standardizing Human Gene No-
menclature has developed a system knownas the International System.
for Human Gene Nomenclature, but this is not universally accepted.
Human genes are generally denoted by italicized symbols composed of
capital letters, sometimes with the addition of an arabic numeral (e.g.
CF, G6PD). Lower-case letters are commonly still used in blood-group
symbols (e.g. Fy, Rh). Phenotype symbols are set in roman type (e.g.
ADA 1).

Human chromosomes may be referred to by number or letter (1-22, X,
Y) or by group (A-G).

For bacteria, genotype symbols usually consist of three italic lower-case
letters (e.g. ara} and phenotype symbols of three roman letters, the first
of which is a capital (e.g. Met).

Restriction enzymes

The generally used nomenclature for restriction enzymes follows, more

15. Non-discriminatory language

Of the examples of discrimination that are most likely to be encoun-
tered in manuscripts, the most common involve discrimination on the
grounds of sex. For this reason, the guidelines presented here concen-
trate largely on means of avoiding overt or implied sex discrimination.
However, examples of racial or cultural discrimination may also be
found, and many of the principles that apply to the elimination of
sex discrimination can also be applied to the eradication of other types

of bias.

Modifying the way people speak and write is not easy, and there is —
and will continue to be — considerable resistance to change. Unless the
use of non-discriminatory language becomes standard practice, how-
ever, the achievement of sexual, racial and cultural equality will be

hindered.

The importance of non-discriminatory language

Women’s role in society can be unconsciously diminished or trivialized
bya failure to mention them specifically in writing—thus implying that
they are less important than men.

By excluding women, sexist language can cause offence. It is the
mistaken belief of many authors that their use of terms such as “man”,
“he” and “him” will be assumed by their readers to include women.
Most readers make no such assumptions. Moreover, the use of exclu-
sively masculine nouns and pronouns to denote both men and women.
can become hopelessly ambiguous and lead to confusion, as in the
following example:

“As for man, he is no different from the rest. His back aches, he

ruptures easily, his women have difficulties in childbirth.”

Further examples demonstrate how sexist writing reinforces many
common but inaccurate concepts of the roles of men and women in
society:

“Research scientists often neglect their wives and children.”

“Tris a great secret of doctors, known only by their wives ... that most
things get better by themselves.”

Non-sexist language — a cumbersome solution?

With care, the results of rewriting text to eliminate discriminatory
language will be no less concise than the original — but will be unam-
biguous and will avoid causing offence. For example:

“He may see himself as the pivot of all that happens. Hallucinations,
especially of hearing, are common and may comment on the patient
or address him.”

is no more cumbersome when modified as follows:
“Patients may see themselves as the pivot of all that happens.
Hallucinations, especially of hearing, are common and may com-
ment on or address patients.”

Similarly:
“Onset of disease and the problem of recognition when the patient
starts to observe changes in his skin (e.g. whitish spots, rashes,
thickening of skin).

Emerging deformities and the problem of uncertainty: patient starts
to feel, and then complain about, the possible effects of the skin
changes on his general well-being.”

can be rendered equally well as follows:

“Onset of disease; problems of recognition when patients start to
observe skin changes...

Emerging deformities; problems of uncertainty; patients start to feel
and then complain about the effects of skin changes on their general
well-being.”

In reporting research of any kind that involves both men and women
and/or children of both sexes, both males and females should be
mentioned in the results. In statistical reports, gross figures should be
broken down by sex unless it has previously been established that there
is no significant difference between the sexes.

Illustrations and photographs for inclusion in publications should be
carefully chosen to ensure that the sexes are equally represented, both
in number and in the roles they fulfil. Women, as well as men, should
be depicted in professional roles and men, as well as women, in caring
roles (such as looking after children).

Non-discriminatory language

The occasional exception

There will be occasions when a term including “man” may be unavoid-
able, even when the context is not limited to men. An obvious
example is “man-made disasters”, for which no generally acceptable
alternative is available. Use of the word “Chairman” rather than the
more general “Chair” or “Chairperson” may be obligatory. It would be
wrong to be over-dogmatic in this area: editors should exercise their
judgement, based on the principles outlined here.

Avoiding other types of discrimination

In assessing whether a particular text could cause offence on racial or
cultural terms, try substituting your own ethnic or cultural group for
that under discussion. If the revised text causes you to feel belittled or
offended, you can safely assume that the original was guilty of bias.

Other forms of language that may cause offence include “ageist” terms
such as “geriatrics”. Avoid this type of discrimination by substituting
less impersonal words such as “older people” or “elderly patients”. Care
should also be taken in describing people with disabilities; suggestions
for appropriate terminology are given in the Guidelines produced by
the Research and Training Center on Independent Living.

People who are ill should be referred to as “patients”: use “diabetic
Pp Pi

patients” or “patients with hypertension” rather than the dehumaniz-
ing “diabetic cases” or “hypertensives”.

Avoid terms such as “the western world” and “Middle (or Far) East”
which imply that everything is viewed from the standpoint of Western
Europe. There is also an increasing tendency for the terms “North” and
“South” to be used to imply a particular stage of development. Usage
of all such terms should be restricted to a geographical context only.
Similarly, refer to “developing countries” rather than to “underdevel-
oped countries” or “the Third World”.

16. Politically and legally sensitive topics

Occasionally, manuscripts contain statements that may cause offence
tocertain Member States, or references to geographical entities whose
name or ownership is disputed. It would be unrealistic to try to provide
a comprehensive list of sensitive topics, and editors need to be alert to
potentially controversial issues. These may include, for example,
terminology that implies a Euro-centric view of the world (Middle
East, Far East), references to countries as underdeveloped, and sugges-
tions that a particular government is corrupt. As a general rule, any
text in a technical publication that describes the workings of, or
criticizes, a particular government or national health service should
have been cleared by the government concerned; this is the responsi-
bility of the technical unit. Brief statements of this nature presented as
examples from particular countries or as attributed views from other
publications are generally acceptable, but editors should always be
aware of the possibility of causing embarrassment to governments and
should clear any doubtful text through LEG.

Political and geographical designations

In general, WHO follows United Nations practice in the field of
geographical terminology. However, when situations are changing fast
it may be impossible for editors to keep up with the latest develop-
ments. If editors have any doubts on the acceptability of a particular
name or designation or the status of a country with respect to member-
ship of WHO, they should check with LEG. (For quick reference, the
Official List of Addresses contains a regularly updated list of Member
States.)

When several Member States are referred to in the same context in
tables, figures or text, and there is no obvious reason for grouping them
together in a particular way (e.g. by perinatal mortality rate or in order
of gross national product), they should be listed in alphabetical order.

Country, state, territory

All WHO publications carry a standard disclaimer regarding the
designation of countries, territories, cities, areas, and their authorities,

Maps

All maps used in WHO publications should follow United Nations
practice with respect to place names and drawing of boundaries. A
copy of the standard United Nations map of the world is with GRA.
Maps for publication should be cleared through LEG if they have not
been prepared by GRA or if editors are in any doubt about particular
borders or areas.

Specific areas
The following are some examples of designations where care is needed.

Taiwan

WHO considers Taiwan as a province of China, under the jurisdiction
of the Chinese Government in Beijing. If it is felt necessary to include
data from Taiwan, it should be referred to as China (Province of
Taiwan) or as Taiwan, China. WHO does not recognize the term
Taiwan, Republic of China.

The term “Persian Gulf” is the standard geographical designation for
the body of water lying between the Arabian peninsula and the Islamic
Republic of Iran. It is recognized, however, that a number of states
prefer the term Arabian Gulf, and in text referring specifically to these
states the term “Gulf” may be used.

United Kingdom

The term United Kingdom refers to the Member State, but care should
be taken in applying it to the legal system or health services, for which
separate provisions exist in England and Wales, Northern Ireland, and
Scotland. When the context is neither political nor legal, as in
addresses and geographical descriptions, the relevant constituent part
of the United Kingdom should be named (e.g. England, Scotland,
Great Britain).

Trade marks and manufacturers’ names

All WHO publications carry a disclaimer concerning the mention of
proprietary products (see section 8). Nevertheless, to avoid the impli-
cation that WHO endorses or recommendsa particular manufacturer's
product (drug, pesticide, item of medical equipment) in preference to
others, generic names should be used instead of trade names whenever
possible. Ifa specific proprietary product must be named ina technical
publication, it should be distinguished by an initial capital letter; it
may be necessary to explain its inclusion if the context is particularly
sensitive.
