who-ed-ize-preferred-ize, not -ise
Where a word may be spelled with -ize or -ise, use -ize: organize, not organise; organization, not organisation. The manual's stated ground is the Greek root and the first spelling in The concise Oxford dictionary. The exception is a word where -ise is not a suffix but part of the root — surprise, comprise, improvise — which keep -ise. This rule is the reason the voice had to be derived rather than assumed: it is commonly believed that WHO house style is -ise, and PR #210 asserted exactly that.		spelling · major
Where there is a choice between using the suffix -ize or -ise (e.g. organize or organise), -ize, derived from the Greek "-izo", is preferred, consistent with the first spelling of such words given in The concise Oxford dictionary. For some words, such as surprise, comprise or improvise, where -ise is not a suffix but part of the root of the word, there is no choice.

who-ed-eponym-no-genitiveCrohn disease, not Crohn's disease
An eponymous name takes no Saxon genitive: Crohn disease, Down syndrome. The manual goes further and asks that eponyms be avoided altogether where a descriptive name exists, on the ground that an eponym carries no information about the disease and may name something different in another country — so a finding here is worth reading as two questions, not one.		terminology · major
Eponymous names give no information about the nature of the disease and different names may be used in different countries. They should be avoided whenever possible. The Saxon genitive ('s) is not necessary when using an eponymous name; e.g. Crohn disease, not Crohn's disease, and Down syndrome, not Down's syndrome.

who-ed-who-capitalizationMember State, the Organization — and headquarters, primary health care
The manual gives two explicit lists rather than a principle, and the two halves are easy to get backwards. Capitalised: Member State, Associate Member, the Organization (meaning WHO), the Constitution, Region in a specific reference, Regional Committee, Regional Director, World Health Assembly, General Programme of Work, Global Strategy for Health for All. Lower case: headquarters, primary health care, health for all, session, resolution, decision, strategy, specialized agency, representatives, committee and commission unless specifically identified, region used generically, and names of seasons. Note the pair the manual calls out itself: member (of a committee) but Member (State).		capitalization · minor
The following words and expressions used frequently within WHO normally require capitalization: Associate Member; Constitution (of WHO); (Eighth) General Programme of Work; Forty-… World Health Assembly; … Member (country); Member State; … the Organization (WHO); Region [of WHO] … / The following words and expressions do not normally require capitalization: commission, committee (unless specifically identified); … health for all; headquarters (WHO); member (of the committee, etc.), but Member (State); … primary health care; … specialized agency
who-pub-tps-931#page-013, p13-14
who-ed-capitals-sparinglyCapitals break the eye; use them for names, not for importance
Proper names, titles and institutions take capitals. Descriptive appellations — administrative posts and the like — do not, unless lower case would read oddly. A trade name takes an initial capital (Pyrex, Vaseline, with the generic preferred where one exists); a Linnaean genus takes one (Staphylococcus aureus, Trypanosoma) while the anglicised common form does not (trypanosome, schistosome). Article, book and journal titles in references are NOT capitalised except where they contain an organization's name.		capitalization · minor
Too many capital letters break the flow of the eye across a sentence and down a column of type. However, proper names, titles and institutions require capitals. Descriptive appellations, such as administrative posts, do not, unless the result would appear odd or confusing. … Generic terms in the Linnaean classification also take an initial capital letter, e.g. Staphylococcus aureus, Trypanosoma, Schistosoma, but trypanosome, schistosome, etc. Article, book and journal titles in references need not be capitalized, e.g. Social science and medicine, except when they contain the name of an association or organization

who-ed-reference-authorsAll authors up to three; et al. from four
List every author when there are three or fewer. From four, give the first author only and add et al. Author names are surname followed by initials with no periods and no comma between them — Cairns RB, DuPont B, Crompton DWT et al.		citation · major
1. List all authors when three or fewer; when four or more, give only first author's name and add et al.
who-pub-tps-931#page-033, p33
who-ed-journal-names-in-fullWrite journal names out in full
No abbreviated journal titles. The manual's reason is worth keeping with the rule, because it decides the borderline cases: it is for readers whose first language is not English, who cannot expand an unfamiliar abbreviation. So 'Bulletin of the World Health Organization', never 'Bull World Health Organ'.		citation · major
2. Write out journal names in full. This is particularly helpful to readers whose mother tongue is not English.
who-pub-tps-931#page-033, p33
who-ed-non-discriminatory-languageRewrite, do not decorate: the non-discriminatory pairs
The manual's own table, which is prescriptive about the TECHNIQUE and not only the vocabulary. Its four general rules are: delete redundant possessive pronouns; rephrase; use the first person plural; and use neutral or equivalent job titles. Its worked substitutions include foreman/workman/office girl/policeman becoming supervisor/worker/secretary or receptionist or office assistant/police officer; 'Man, mankind' and 'the average man' rephrased; 'show the mother how to cook' becoming 'show members of the family', on the stated ground that women are not assumed to do the household chores; 'ask mothers to bring their children' becoming 'ask parents', because men have responsibility for children too; and 'Mrs John Smith' becoming 'Jane Smith'.		terminology · major
Common discriminatory phrases / Useful solutions / Useful general rules … Delete redundant possessive pronouns … Use the first person plural … Foreman, workman, office girl, policeman → Supervisor, worker, secretary, receptionist, office assistant, police officer → Use neutral or equivalent job titles … Show the mother how to cook nutritious meals → Show members of the family how to cook nutritious meals → Do not assume that women always do the household chores … Ask mothers to bring their children to the clinic each month → Ask parents to bring their children to the clinic each month → Remember that men have responsibility for children too
who-pub-tps-931#page-070, p70-72
who-ed-rewriting-stays-conciseThe rewrite is not more cumbersome — that is the manual's own claim
The manual anticipates the objection that non-discriminatory phrasing is clumsy and answers it with worked pairs of equal length, moving to the plural rather than to 'he or she': 'He may see himself as the pivot of all that happens … may comment on the patient or address him' becomes 'Patients may see themselves as the pivot of all that happens … may comment on or address patients'. So a reviewer should reject 'he or she' and 's/he' patches and ask for the plural or a rephrase. It also asks that research involving both sexes report both, and that gross figures in statistical reports be broken down by sex unless no significant difference has already been established.		register · minor
With care, the results of rewriting text to eliminate discriminatory language will be no less concise than the original — but will be unambiguous and will avoid causing offence. … In reporting research of any kind that involves both men and women and/or children of both sexes, both males and females should be mentioned in the results. In statistical reports, gross figures should be broken down by sex unless it has previously been established that there is no significant difference between the sexes.
who-pub-tps-931#page-069, p69
who-ed-country-names-are-volatileCountry names are checked, not remembered
The manual is explicit that country names and their spellings change 'at an alarming rate' and names the office that holds the current list, with full, short and adjectival forms. The rule for an author or an agent is therefore procedural: do not spell a Member State's name from memory or from an older document in the same corpus. This is judgement-only by construction — no pattern can know which name was current when a passage was written.		terminology · minor
Webster's new geographical dictionary … can be used as a guide to the spelling of common geographical entities. However, country names and their spelling, in particular, can change at an alarming rate. The Computer-Assisted Translation and Terminology subunit (CTT), which coordinates with the Office of the Legal Counsel (LEG), can provide up-to-date information on the names of United Nations and WHO Member States, including their full, short and adjectival/attributive forms.

who-ed-spelling-generalFollow The concise Oxford dictionary's first spelling — and Annex 1 where WHO differs
The general spelling rule the manual's Annex 1 opens with: take the spelling The concise Oxford dictionary gives first, or the one a variant entry refers you to. Annex 1 then lists WHO's own exceptions and the words that most often cause doubt; the rules who-ed-spelling-annex-p088 to p102 carry that list, one rule per printed page, each quoting its page. Where a word is not listed, this rule decides.		spelling · minor
The general rule in WHO is to follow the spelling given in the latest edition of The concise Oxford dictionary; where it gives alternative spellings or forms, use the one given first (e.g. fulfil, fulfill) or the one to which you are referred from an entry giving a variant spelling (e.g. "tire, US var. of tyre": use "tyre").
