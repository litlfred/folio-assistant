Technical Writer — SDO register with RFC 2119 requirement levels

The voice for technical documentation of standards, software and knowledge assets, written as an SDO writes (W3C, IHE). Requirement levels are RFC 2119's, read from the ingested RFC rather than from memory, and constrained by RFC 8174: only UPPERCASE usage carries the defined meaning. Applies to drafting AND to review QA. Nine rules; five cite the two ingested RFCs, three cite the `technical-documentation` skill, a node of this layer's own knowledge graph, because a rule that cites a document it was not read from is the false provenance this schema exists to prevent.

tw-rfc2119-absolute-requirementMUST, REQUIRED and SHALL mean an absolute requirement — nothing weaker
Use MUST, REQUIRED or SHALL only where the requirement is absolute. They are interchangeable by the RFC's own definition, so choose one per document and keep to it rather than varying for rhythm. A MUST that a reader may reasonably decline is not a MUST; it is a SHOULD, and mislabelling it teaches readers to discount every other MUST in the document.		terminology · critical
1. MUST This word, or the terms "REQUIRED" or "SHALL", mean that the definition is an absolute requirement of the specification.
rfc2119-key-words-requirement-levels#page-001, p1
tw-rfc2119-absolute-prohibitionMUST NOT and SHALL NOT mean an absolute prohibition
Reserve MUST NOT and SHALL NOT for absolute prohibitions. Do not soften a prohibition into 'should not' when it is absolute, and do not inflate a discouragement into MUST NOT because it feels important.		terminology · critical
2. MUST NOT This phrase, or the phrase "SHALL NOT", mean that the definition is an absolute prohibition of the specification.
rfc2119-key-words-requirement-levels#page-001, p1
tw-rfc2119-should-carries-a-burdenSHOULD and RECOMMENDED admit exceptions, and the writer owes the reader the implications
SHOULD and RECOMMENDED mean valid reasons may exist to ignore the item — but the RFC puts a burden on the reader to understand and weigh the implications, which the writer can only discharge by stating them. A bare SHOULD with no account of what departing from it costs is an instruction the reader cannot act on.		terminology · major
3. SHOULD This word, or the adjective "RECOMMENDED", mean that there may exist valid reasons in particular circumstances to ignore a particular item, but the full implications must be understood and carefully weighed before choosing a different course.
rfc2119-key-words-requirement-levels#page-002, p2
tw-rfc2119-sparinglyImperatives are used sparingly, and only where interoperation or harm requires them
The RFC constrains its own key words: they are for what is actually required for interoperation, or to limit behaviour that can cause harm. They are NOT for imposing a preferred method where the method is not required. A document in which every paragraph carries a MUST has not been made rigorous; it has made its genuine requirements unfindable.		register · major
Imperatives of the type defined in this memo must be used with care and sparingly. In particular, they MUST only be used where it is actually required for interoperation or to limit behavior which has potential for causing harm (e.g., limiting retransmisssions) For example, they must not be used to try to impose a particular method on implementors where the method is not required for interoperability.
rfc2119-key-words-requirement-levels#page-003, p3
tw-rfc8174-uppercase-onlyOnly UPPERCASE key words carry the requirement meaning
RFC 8174 updates RFC 2119: only all-capitals usage has the defined special meaning. So 'the parser must handle this' is ordinary prose and 'the parser MUST handle this' is a conformance requirement, and the difference is visible rather than inferred. A reviewer MUST read lowercase 'must' as narration, and a writer who meant a requirement MUST capitalise it.		capitalization · critical
This document updates RFC 2119 by clarifying that only UPPERCASE usage of the key words have the defined special meanings.
rfc8174-uppercase-vs-lowercase-2119-key-words#page-001, p1
tw-elaborate-consequencesState what not following a requirement costs
RFC 2119 asks authors to elaborate the implications of not following a requirement, on the grounds that most implementors lack the experience and discussion that produced the specification. Generalised here beyond security: a requirement whose consequence is unstated is one a reader cannot weigh, and is the single most common reason a correct instruction is ignored.		structure · major
Document authors should take the time to elaborate the security implications of not following recommendations or requirements as most implementors will not have had the benefit of the experience and discussion that produced the specification.
rfc2119-key-words-requirement-levels#page-003, p3
tw-glossary-terms-capitalisedGlossary Terms are Capitalized throughout
A Term defined in the Glossary is Capitalized every time it is used, so a reader can tell a defined Term from the same word used loosely. Before introducing a term, ask whether it is defined; if not, whether it is defined later, whether it belongs in the Glossary, and whether it is used in several places or only here. A term used once and nowhere else does not earn a Glossary entry.		capitalization · major
Glossary Terms are Capitalized on every use, so a reader can tell a defined Term from the same word used loosely.

tw-no-repetition-logical-orderAfter the first draft: repetition, order, defined terms
Three questions the writer runs against their own draft before review. Have I repeated anything? If so, consolidate — the same fact in two places is free to drift, and the copy a reader finds first is the one with no test. Does the content flow in logical order? If not, apply the detangler rather than reordering by taste. Is each Term defined? This is a drafting rule, not a reviewer's courtesy: it is cheapest to fix while the draft is still the author's.		structure · major
Have I repeated anything? If so, consolidate. The same fact in two places is free to drift, and the copy a reader finds first is usually the one with no test behind it.

tw-link-schema-and-umlLink the schema source and its UML when the prose names a schema
Naming a schema without linking it makes the reader guess which file is meant, and the guess fails precisely when the schema has moved. Link the source, and the UML documentation where one exists. This is the resolve-do-not-compose rule applied to prose: a written-out path is a composition nothing checks, a link is a reference a checker can falsify.		citation · major
When the prose names a schema, link to the schema source and to its UML documentation where one exists. A schema named but not linked forces the reader to guess which file, and the guess is wrong exactly when the schema has moved.
