Peter, I wonder who you think might worry most about this, you, your father,
mother, your grandparents or your brother?

I don’t worry about it. My mother does and so does granny. She only wants
me and my brother to have kids.

So, Peter, you think that your granny puts pressure on your mother?

She is always worrying about those things.

Mrs J., did you know that Peter thinks you and your mother are most worried
about this and Peter least?

I didn’t realize that was how he saw it.

Mr J., what is your greatest worry today?

That Peter won’t work at schoo! because he won’t see the point.

What do you think about what Dad has said?

He just worries that we get a good job. I find work boring at school.

What would you prefer to be doing?

I don’t know. There isn’t any point, is there, if I’m going to die. You die of
AIDS, don’t you?

Yes, you can die of AIDS. Is dying something that worries you?

Ï don’t worry about dying. I won’t know about anything then. I just want to
do what I want.

What do you want Peter?

Not to be nagged all day.

Just say, Peter, that you keep as well as you are for a good number of years.
How would you fill your time?

I could get a job.

Mr J., what is your view about what Peter has been saying? Do any of us
know exactly how much time we have got? We could have an accident
tomorrow. Do you think that you could discuss with Peter how he might do
what he wants, but also show him how difficult it is to get jobs without any
qualification?

Mrs J., we have heard from Peter that he is not worried about dying. Nor is he
worried that he might not marry and have children. He gave me the idea that
there are many people who can’t have children. Some never find partners,
some have other illnesses, and others are infertile. So, although this is your
worry now, Peter’s worries seem different.
