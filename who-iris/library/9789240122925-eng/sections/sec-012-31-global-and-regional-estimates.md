3.1 Global and regional estimates
Member States have been sharing stock data for several occupations of the health and care 
workforce. Table 1 presents the list of occupational groups in the NHWA data platform, 
along with the frequency of countries which have shared stock data for these occupations. 
Data is available for occupations such as medical doctors, nursing personnel, dentists, 
pharmacists, midwifery personnel, physiotherapists, dental assistants and therapists, dental 
prosthetic technicians, medical imaging and therapeutic equipment technicians, medical 
and pathology laboratory technicians, environmental and occupational health and hygiene 
professionals, pharmaceutical technicians and assistants, medical and pathology laboratory 
scientists, community health workers (CHWs), paramedical practitioners, administrative staff, 
optometrists and ophthalmic technicians for over 100 countries, while data is available for 
personal care workers for nearly 100 countries.

3. Health workforce global and regional levels and trends
In 2016, the GSHRH estimated the shortage of health workers to exceed 18 million by 2030 (5). 
The adoption of the GSHRH and through it the progressive implementation of the NHWA 
has facilitated the obtention of reliable and annually updated information on the stock, 
demographics, distribution and mobility patterns of health workers to address national, regional 
and global level HWF challenges. An assessment using NHWA data showed that the global 
shortage reduced from 20 million in 2013 to 15 million health workers in 2020, with a projected 
decrease to 10 million by 2030 (18), while a recent analysis showed an upward adjustment in 
the projected shortage of health workers by 2030 to 11.1 million (45). Current health workers 
stock estimates (Table 2) almost break even with the 2030 projected stock values in the GSHRH 
and will exceed them as trend continues. Though there has been a tremendous increase in 
HWF globally, WHO regions with the highest burden of disease continue to have the lowest 
proportion of HWF to deliver the health services.

Latest data available up to 2024 for 174 countries estimate approximately 1.7 million 
physiotherapists, with 53 countries in the European Region and 25 countries from the Region 
of the Americas constituting close to 76% of the personnel. Latest available data from 108 
countries presents an estimated 4 million CHWs, of which 44 countries in the African Region, 
8 countries from the South-East Asia Region and 15 countries from the Western Pacific Region 
constitute 23%, 41% and 22% of the personnel respectively. In this report, we will remain 
focused on the SDG 3.c.1 occupations.
Fig. 12 to Fig. 15 represent the density of health workers in different occupations across 
countries, based on latest available data. Some countries in the Region of the Americas, 
European Region and Western Pacific Region have higher density of health workers across all 
occupations, while almost all of the countries in the African Region have much lower density 
of health workers.

Estimates from the latest available data (Table 3) show that the global average density of 
nursing and midwifery personnel is two times that of medical doctors. The highest densities 
of medical doctors, nursing and midwifery personnel, dentists and pharmacists are in the 
European Region followed by the Region of the Americas, while the African Region has the 
lowest density across health occupations.
The data presents stark inequalities at the global and regional levels. The lowest density of 
medical doctors exists in the African Region at 0.4 per 10 000 population and the highest which 
is 215 times more exists in the European Region. The difference between the lowest and the 
highest densities is over 109, 168 and 234 times for nursing and midwifery personnel, dentists 
and pharmacists respectively.
Estimates across income groups show that density of health workers is highly proportionate 
with income groups. High-income countries (HICs) have the highest density of health workers 
while low-income countries (LICs) and lower middle income countries (LMICs) have the lowest 
density, across all health occupations.
