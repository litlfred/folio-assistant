NHWA methodology
NHWA data cycle
Following the principles of the progressive implementation of the NHWA, countries are 
encouraged to report updated and most recent HWF statistics to WHO. This is in direct 
response to the World Health Assembly Resolution 69.19 urging all countries to report HWF 
data to WHO.

While the NHWA data platform remains open for data entry throughout the year, countries are 
encouraged to channel their data sharing efforts during specified periods to facilitate reporting 
to WHO and the UN SDGs (Fig. A2.1). In addition to data sharing by countries through the 
NHWA data platform, the NHWA Secretariat also integrates HWF data reported by countries 
through the Joint Data Collection on Non-Monetary Health Care Statistics by OECD, Eurostat 
and WHO Regional Office for Europe. Since the definitions of the occupations and indicators 
are aligned with the NHWA, these data are automatically uploaded onto the NHWA data 
platform each year. Furthermore, HWF data from other national sources such as labour force 
surveys (collated  and published by ILO) and census (collated and published by IPUMS) are also 
periodically analyzed and incorporated on the NHWA data platform.
Following the data entry period from March to June, all data in the platform is processed 
and undergoes extensive data quality checks to identify outliers, missing data points and 
other data quality concerns. The identified data points are then brought to the attention of 
the focal points and WHO country and regional colleagues for immediate action to resolve 
them. Throughout the data entry and data processing stages, the NHWA Secretariat provides 
technical support to focal points through webinars and virtual meetings. The validated data 
in the NHWA data platform is then processed for data release to the NHWA data portal, GHO, 
World Health Data Hub and the UN SDG database.
The process of data gathering, data quality checks, data validation and data dissemination is led 
by the NHWA Secretariat located within the Health Workforce Data and Learning Analytics Unit 
at WHO headquarters, in close collaboration with regional counterparts. The NHWA Secretariat, 
in collaboration with regional offices and partners, also provides technical support to countries 
to strengthen the collection, analysis and use of HWF data and information, bolstered by robust 
national HRHIS, and drive evidence-based HWF planning and policy-making.
Density estimates
The figures for number of medical doctors include generalist medical practitioners and 
specialist medical practitioners. The ISCO-08 codes included here are 2211, 2212. 
The figures for number of nursing and midwifery personnel include nursing personnel and 
midwifery personnel, whenever available. Due to varied education pathways and national 
occupational classifications, in many countries nurses trained with midwifery skills perform 
the functions of a midwife and may be counted as midwives. The ISCO-08 codes included here 
are 2221, 2222, 3221, 3222.

The figures for number of dentists include dentists. The ISCO-08 code included here is 2261. 
The figures for number of pharmacists include pharmacists. The ISCO-08 code included here 
is 2262. 
The global and regional average densities were estimated as the population weighted average 
of the national densities. In general, the denominator data for workforce density (i.e. national 
population estimates) are obtained from the population estimates prepared by the Population 
Division of the Department of Economic and Social Affairs of the UN Secretariat, of which the 
latest edition is the 2024 revision (46). 
Following the principles of progressive implementation of NHWA in countries, focal points 
periodically update latest and/or trend data, thereby improving the HWF data quality. Due 
to these updates in data, the global and regional estimates should not be compared to prior 
estimates published elsewhere.
Depending on the nature of the original data source, it may include only practising health 
workers or all those in the health occupation who are licensed to practice. Data on health 
workers tend to be more complete for the public health sector and may underestimate the 
active workforce in the private, military, nongovernmental organization and faith-based health 
sectors. Due to the differences in data sources, considerable variability may remain across 
countries in the coverage, periodicity, quality and completeness of the original data.
