1.1 Historical overview of health workforce data 
strengthening efforts
Since the onset of the 21st century, there has been an increasing impetus on the central role of 
health workers in developing and ensuring robust health systems and on the need for countries 
to mobilize resources to strengthen their health workforce (HWF), and in particular to generate 
robust HWF data and evidence (Fig. 1). The Joint Learning Initiative – a consortium of more 
than 100 health leaders – recognized in 2004 that the health crisis can only be overcome if 
countries develop national human resources for health (HRH) strategic plans and increase their 
investments in HWF education, employment and retention, yet acknowledged that HWF data 
and research is sparse and called on countries to strengthen the evidence base and bridge 
the knowledge-action gap (1). The World Health Organization (WHO) presented a global HWF 
profile in the World health report 2006 and called for national leadership and global solidarity 
to create significant structural improvements in all countries (2). Building on this report, WHO 
established a Global health workforce statistics database in the public domain, with annual 
data updates by the WHO Secretariat on HWF stock data by health occupations and limited 
demographics data. Subsequently, WHO collaborated with the United States Agency for 
International Development (USAID) and the World Bank to develop a handbook that documented 
methodologies for HWF monitoring and evaluation and shared country experiences (3). In 2013, 
the report titled “A universal truth: no health without a workforce”, which was commissioned 
by WHO and the Global Health Workforce Alliance (GHWA) Secretariat to consolidate the latest 
information available on HRH and inform the global community on how to accelerate progress 
towards universal health coverage (UHC), it presented a 10-point agenda for policy-makers, one 
of which was to build the data, evidence base and strategic intelligence required to implement 
and monitor policy objectives and to effectively manage their HWF (4).

In 2016, WHO’s Global strategy on human resources for health: workforce 2030 (GSHRH) 
outlined the policy agenda to ensure a HWF that is fit for purpose to attain UHC and the 
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) (5). It acknowledged the importance of better HRH 
data and evidence as a critical enabler to enhance advocacy, multi-sectoral dialogue, planning, 
policy-making, governance and accountability at national, regional and global levels. Adopted 
by the Sixty-ninth World Health Assembly in May 2016 under Resolution 69.19, the GSHRH 
urged Member States to “implement policy options towards consolidating a core set of HRH 
data ... as well as progressive implementation of national health workforce accounts (NHWA), 
to support national policy and planning ...” (6). One of the GSHRH’s global milestones for 2020 
was that “all countries are making progress on sharing data on HRH through NHWA and submit 
core indicators to the WHO Secretariat annually” (5). During the COVID-19 crisis, many countries 
around the world faced disruptions in the delivery of essential health services and challenges 
with emergency preparedness and response. This highlighted the need for countries to develop 
a multidisciplinary workforce that is capable of delivering the essential public health functions 
and to monitor their national workforce capacity (7).
