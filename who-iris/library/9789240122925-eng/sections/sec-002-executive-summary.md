Over the last 20 years, there has been an increasing realization of the importance of having 
reliable and up-to-date data on the health and care workforce at the national level in order 
to understand the health labour market and to inform the development, implementation and 
review of health workforce (HWF) policies and plans. The Joint Learning Initiative in 2004, 
the World health report in 2006 and the report titled “A universal truth: no health without 
a workforce” in 2013 highlighted the need for countries to invest resources in strengthening 
HWF data and evidence to effectively manage and monitor their HWF. The Global Strategy on 
Human Resources for Health: Workforce 2030 (GSHRH) outlines the policy agenda to ensure 
a HWF that is fit for purpose to attain universal health coverage (UHC), health security and 
the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). One of its four objectives is to strengthen data 
on human resources for health (HRH), for monitoring and ensuring accountability for the 
implementation of national and regional strategies, and the GSHRH. Accordingly, the National 
Health Workforce Accounts (NHWA) was adopted as an important means to support countries 
in their national HWF policy-making and planning and to track their efforts towards UHC, the 
SDGs, the global milestones of the GSHRH and other health objectives. 
 
The National health workforce accounts: health workforce levels and trends 2026 intends to 
provide readers a snapshot of the status of NHWA implementation and the global and regional 
HWF levels and trends based on official country data shared by national focal points to the 
World Health Organization (WHO) through the WHO NHWA data platform. 
Results and Key Statistics:
1.	 NHWA implementation:
Since the launch of the NHWA in 2017, there has been a tremendous improvement in the 
availability and quality of HWF data at national, regional and global levels, as a result of 
Member States’ efforts to strengthen their HRH situation, develop robust HRH information 
systems, improve their data and evidence, and use these data to inform evidence-based HRH 
plans and policies. 
•	 206 countries, territories and areas (including 98% of WHO Member States) have 
appointed focal points who coordinate national multi-sectoral efforts to compile and 
share HWF data annually on the NHWA data platform. 
•	 178 countries (92%) reported data on the platform during the 2025 NHWA data cycle; 192 
countries (99%) have data available for the last five years (2020–2024).
•	 186 countries (96%) have data available on the NHWA platform for more than 10 indicators 
or sub-indicators. 
•	 192 countries (99%) have stock data on the NHWA platform for more than 5 occupations.
vii
•	 More than 100 countries have reported data across key HWF topics covering density and 
distribution, such as stock, activity level, graduates and HWF distribution by sex, age 
group, place of birth, place of training and facility ownership.
•	 Data availability for key HWF topics has increased almost 20-fold from 2018 to 2025.
2.	 Health workforce global and regional levels and trends:
Stock data for several occupational groups of the health and care workforce have been reported 
by more than 100 countries. Analysis of selected countries which reported data for more than 
20 occupations in the last five years reveals that the share of different health occupations 
varies by country, depending on the national health systems agenda, priorities, settings and 
service delivery models.
According to the latest available data, substantial disparities persist in HWF density, both by 
WHO region and by World Bank income group. 
•	 The highest densities of medical doctors, nursing and midwifery personnel, dentists and 
pharmacists are in the European Region followed by the Region of the Americas, while the 
African Region has the lowest health worker density across all the above occupations.
•	 Density of health workers is highly proportionate with income groups, with high-income 
countries (HICs) having the highest density of health workers and low-income countries 
(LICs) having the lowest density, across all occupations.
There has been a 52% increase in global HWF density from 44.8 per 10 000 population reported 
in 2006 to 67.9 reported in 2025, but variations in this trend can be observed by occupation and 
by WHO region. 
3.	 Population and health workforce ageing:
The proportion of the global population aged 65 years and above is about one in 10 in 2024 – 
with HICs having the highest share (nearly one out of five) – and this is projected to become 
about one in eight by the year 2030, increasing by 21% globally.
The share of workers aged 55 years and above (i.e. those who are expected to retire on average 
in the upcoming 10 years) varies by occupation and income group. 
•	 HICs have a higher share of health workers aged 55 years and above compared to LICs, 
with nearly one out of three medical doctors retiring within the next 10 years in HICs.
The pattern of ageing workforce (the share of health workers aged 55 years and above is greater 
than the share of those less than 35 years) is marked for medical doctors in HICs and to a lower 
extent in upper middle-income countries (UMICs).
viii
The ageing of the population would induce an increased health need, hence it is not sufficient 
for countries to maintain the current HWF density. 
•	 67 countries would not be able to maintain their age-standardized HWF density of 2024 
by the year 2030.
•	 An additional 832 000 health workers are needed just to maintain the age-standardized 
HWF density, primarily in HICs.
