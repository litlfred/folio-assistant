Health products pass through multiple supply chain 
partners and processes as they move from manufacturers to 
end-users. For transactions to be seamless, all stakeholders 
must consistently identify health products using 
standardized information.
For example, if a country’s central warehouse receives a 
shipment of vaccines with identifiers that do not match its 
standardized data, manual reconciliation will be required. 
This can lead to delays, errors, and distribution disruptions, 
ultimately impacting health outcomes.
Uniform and standardized product information builds trust 
among supply chain partners. By matching standardized 
data with physical products, partners can validate the 
source of health products. Most importantly, accurate, 
reliable, and consistent product information enables health 
facilities and individuals to verify product authenticity and 
use them with confidence.
What is Product Master Data?
Data plays a crucial role in the efficient management of 
health supply chains, ensuring that essential health 
products reach the right location at the right time and cost. 
High-quality, timely data provides supply chain leaders with 
critical insights, enabling informed decision-making that 
improves health outcomes.
Product master data consists of standardized information 
that accurately describes a health product’s characteristics. 
This data remains consistent across all instances of a 
product. Examples include product description, dosage, 
pharmaceutical strength, manufacturer details, and shelf 
life. In contrast, transactional data—such as batch numbers, 
manufacturing dates, and serial numbers—varies with each 
specific instance of the product.

Why is Product Master Data important?
Product master data is central to all operational processes 
across supply chain partners. It ensures that each entity 
uniformly understands the transactional information 
exchanged throughout the supply chain. Acting as a 
common language, product master data links all supply 
chain data—including orders, shipments, and inventory—
from manufacturing to dispensing. This standardization 
helps supply chain partners, processes, and systems 
interoperate effectively. Similarly, facility master data 
plays a critical role in enabling national supply chain 
partners to coordinate the delivery of health products 
to the correct locations.

Health products that are inconsistently identified by 
different supply chain partners create barriers to data 
sharing, interpretation, and interoperability. Inconsistent 
product information leads to inefficiencies such as 
duplicate datasets for the same product, increased manual 
reconciliation efforts, and data quality issues. As shown in 
Figure 3, variations in product identification across the 
supply chain hinder seamless information exchange, 
reducing end-to-end visibility and complicating product 
tracking and tracing.
Digital implementation investment guide (DIIG)
Given the critical role of master data in the health supply 
chain, adopting a standardized approach to product master 
data management is essential. This handbook provides 
guidance on implementing standardized product master 
data management. It has been developed using the 
framework and approaches from the Digital 
implementation investment guide (DIIG).
The DIIG supports governments and technical partners in 
planning digital health implementations that align with 
national health system goals. This handbook 
complements the DIIG by providing a structured approach 
to implementing a national product catalogue (NPC) for 
standardized product master data management. 
Standardized product master data enables interoperability 
between digital health systems, regulatory frameworks, 
and supply chain platforms.
Effective master data management helps integrate health 
information with supply chain data, improving key areas 
such as:
•	
Product recalls
•	
Detection of falsified products
•	
Pharmacovigilance
•	
Monitoring Adverse effects following immunisation (AEFI)
By leveraging standardized product master data, health 
systems can enhance data accuracy, improve decision-
making, and strengthen overall supply chain efficiency.

SMART Guidelines
This handbook is developed to complement the WHO’s 
Standards-based, Machine-readable, Adaptive, 
Requirements-based, and Testable (SMART) Guidelines 
initiative by providing guidance on NPC implementations 
that align with digital health implementations.
SMART Guidelines are a structured approach to 
integrating recommended public health and clinical 
practices into digital systems. They represent the WHO’s 
approach to systematising and accelerating the consistent 
application of recommended, life-saving interventions 
in the digital age.
SMART Guidelines offer a comprehensive set of reusable 
digital health components, following a five-step model that 
progressively increases in complexity to advance the 
adoption of best clinical and data practices. The five 
knowledge layers include:
1.	
L1 – Narrative: enhanced clinical, public health 
guidelines and structured data recommendations.
2.	
L2 – Operational: Digital Adaptation Kits (DAKs) for 
local implementation.
3.	
L3 – Machine-Readable: guidelines in structured, 
computable formats.
4.	
L4 – Executable: reference applications and 
interoperable digital services.
5.	
L5 – Dynamic: precision health models for evolving 
health system needs.
These layers guide stakeholders on translating 
recommendations into specifications and standards, 
technical specialists on integrating recommendations into 
updatable digital systems, and countries on localising, 
institutionalising, and updating digital systems while 
maintaining consistency with evidence-based 
recommendations (Figure 5).15

Effective healthcare delivery relies on the timely availability 
of high-quality health products. Standardized product 
master data enhances the interoperability of health, 
regulatory, and digital supply chain systems, ensuring 
seamless data exchange and promoting the timely supply 
of essential health products.
This handbook complements the digital capabilities 
supported by SMART Guidelines by providing guidance on 
L1 adaptations for the management of standardized 
product master data.
Digital health supply chain (DHSC) 
architectural components
The Digital Transformation Handbook for Health Supply 
Chain Architecture16 outlines an architectural framework 
that includes various components such as foundational, 
data services, interoperability, and health supply chain 
information systems. Foundational architectural 
components, such as facility registry, product registry, 
and supplier registry, play a crucial role in establishing 
an effective DHSC.
These components ensure accurate and timely data 
exchange, promoting seamless integration and 
interoperability across various health information 
systems. The product registry, in particular, is essential for 
maintaining standardized product master data, facilitating 
efficient supply chain operations, and enhancing 
healthcare delivery.
The primary focus of this handbook is to offer guidance on 
the implementation and management of the foundational 
product registry component. Although it predominantly 
addresses health products, it does not cover specific 
considerations for medical devices. Future publications are 
expected to provide dedicated guidelines for medical 
devices and other products requiring specialised attention.

How to use this handbook 
This handbook provides a stepwise approach to 
implementing standardized NPC and product master 
data management processes.
•	
Chapter 1 outlines guidance on establishing the 
necessary regulatory framework to support and 
promote product master data standardization.
•	
Chapter 2 addresses policies and considerations 
for product master data management.
•	
Chapter 3 presents technology specifications
and implementation considerations.
•	
Chapter 4 highlights the importance of robust change 
management and governance.
•	
Chapter 5 explores approaches for building human 
resource capacity to sustainably manage NPC.
•	
Chapter 6 provides details on WHO hosted instance 
of the open-source product catalogue management 
tool (PCMT).
•	
Chapter 7 explains how countries can share data 
as a global good to WHO’s hosted instance and 
benefit from aggregated product master data.
How this handbook was developed 
This handbook was developed from the experiences and 
feedback of a variety of experts compiled through:
•	
Stakeholder consultations were conducted through 
a series of in-country and virtual sessions with 
stakeholders who have supported DHSC 
implementations in countries such as Ethiopia, Malawi, 
Nigeria, Rwanda, and Zambia. These discussions 
focused on identifying challenges related to product 
master data management and data standardization, 
exploring country approaches to aligning with global 
standards, and capturing lessons learned from 
country experiences.
•	
DHSC Small Working Group (DHSC SWG): a subgroup 
under the Digital Health and Interoperability Working 
Group (DH&I WG). The DH&I WG is a volunteer 
community of practice committed to strengthening 
health systems through the appropriate and 
responsible use of digital health technologies. Its 
membership includes over 250 individuals from 
governments, donors, multilateral organizations, 
academia, the private sector, and Non-Governmental 
Organizations (NGOs). The DHSC SWG members met 
monthly to review the handbook and provide input 
as individual experts, collaboratively shaping its 
structure and content.  
•	
Insights from NPC Implementations: practical 
experiences of NPCs across different contexts were 
analysed and integrated.
•	
Alignment with Established Frameworks: the steps 
outlined in this handbook are informed by the 
Principles for Digital Development (Annex 1). Some 
methodologies and templates have been adapted from 
established sources such as:
-	
DIIG
-	
WHO/ITU National eHealth Strategy Toolkit
-	
Supply chain information systems maturity 
model (SCISMM)
-	
OpenHIE Framework
Decision-making was collaborative and consensus-based, 
with stakeholder feedback synthesized and validated through 
DHSC SWG meetings. Drafts were iteratively reviewed, revised, 
and agreed upon to ensure diverse perspectives were 
reflected and aligned with global frameworks.

When to use this handbook
This handbook would be a useful resource in the 
following scenarios.
•	
When establishing standardized product master data 
management to support interoperability of health 
supply chain systems and processes. 
•	
When taking a comprehensive approach to digitalizing 
supply chain processes such as receiving and shipping 
with adoption of automatic identification and data 
capture (AIDC) utilizing globally standardized data 
carriers and barcode labels.
•	
When there are disparate and varied sets of 
product information including identifiers managed 
across different health, regulatory and supply 
chain entities, processes and systems, 
hindering interoperability. 
•	
When investing in new supply chain and regulatory 
information systems that need to interoperate and 
exchange data.
•	
When developing a holistic DHSC strategy 
and architecture.
