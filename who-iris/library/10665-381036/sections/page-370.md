6. Author’s conclusions 
6.1 Implications for practice  
LC-aNAAT on respiratory sample is often the initial test to detect tuberculosis disease. Urine LF-LAM is 
an add on test that is in combination with clinical judgement and other tests (parallel use) for the 
diagnosis of HIV-associated tuberculosis. Respiratory LC-aNAAT and urine LF-LAM may be 
complementary when performed as part of a parallel testing strategy to diagnose all forms of HIV-
associated tuberculosis. Reasons for this observed increase in diagnostic sensitivity with addition of 
urine LF-LAM could be its ease of use, ability to be performed on non-sputum samples (for patients 
unable to produce sputum), and ability to identify extrapulmonary or disseminated forms of tuberculosis 
as a non-site-specific test, despite relatively modest individual test sensitivity.  
However, the magnitude of gain in sensitivity should be weighed against the loss of specificity, in 
scenarios with varying prevalence. Especially in low-prevalence settings, using the tests in parallel may 
lead to a large increase in false positive results and over treatment, whereas in high-prevalence settings 
with limited patient follow-up and extrapulmonary tuberculosis investigation, the benefit of identifying 
additional patients with tuberculosis at the point-of-care likely outweighs the relatively lower risk of 
overtreatment of others. Other consequences of false positive results are delayed alternative diagnosis, 
likelihood of anxiety and morbidity caused by additional testing, unnecessary treatment, and possible 
adverse events; possible stigma associated with a diagnosis of tuberculosis. 
The use of LF-LAM in addition to LC-aNAAT can facilitate rapid diagnosis, which is crucial for initiating 
timely treatment, especially for people with HIV who are at a higher risk of tuberculosis-related 
morbidity and mortality. Moreover, our findings suggest that the parallel test strategy may be 
particularly useful to increase sensitivity in settings with seriously ill, lower CD4 counts, and in inpatient 
settings compared to outpatient settings. However, clinicians must consider the risk of interpreting a 
negative result for the combination. The consequences of false-negative results are increased risk of 
morbidity and mortality, delayed treatment initiation, and the continued risk of tuberculosis 
transmission.  
Overall, the parallel use of LC-aNAAT and LF-LAM in inpatients with HIV has an uncertain effect on 
mortality at eight weeks, but likely increases the proportion of people diagnosed with tuberculosis, 
increases the proportion with a microbiological test positive tuberculosis diagnosis, and probably 
increases the proportion of those treated for tuberculosis, compared to a testing strategy with LC-
aNAAT alone. However, the body of evidence on the impact of parallel testing was limited by few trials 
with some concerns about indirectness and unexplained heterogeneity, leading to a lack of certainty 
especially regarding the mortality outcome.  
6.2 Implications for research  
In this review, only two studies reported the accuracy of a parallel strategy as fully paired comparative 
diagnostic accuracy studies. Future studies should report the accuracy of the combination of tests as 
well as that of individual tests. Studies in settings outside sub-Saharan Africa are lacking as is a focus on 
adolescents aged 10 to 18 years. For a non-site-specific test like LF-LAM, it will be important to use a 
reference standard that includes at least two different specimens (e.g. sputum, blood, and urine), and in 
addition, for presumed extrapulmonary tuberculosis, appropriate specimens from the suspected sites of 
involvement. When evaluating a parallel test strategy with an assay that is not site-specific, future 
studies may consider systematic sampling of multiple sites to improve the quality of the reference 
standard. Moreover, future studies should include patients unable to expectorate sputum or urine in the 
analysis of parallel testing and report on the ratio of unavailable sample as well as diagnostic yield, 
which accounts for sample availability.  
Our data highlight the urgent need for implementation research to understand barriers and enablers 
that affect clinical decision making and thus the impact of diagnostic test strategies for tuberculosis.
