1. BACKGROUND   
1.1 Target condition being diagnosed  
Tuberculosis remains a challenge to diagnose particularly in people with HIV. Diagnosis is often 
missed or delayed resulting in worse prognosis and patient outcomes. Globally, tuberculosis resulted 
in 1.1 million deaths among people without HIV and 167,000 deaths among people with HIV in 2022, 
and is the leading cause of death among people with HIV [Organization 2023].  
Tuberculosis predominantly affects the lungs as pulmonary tuberculosis, but can affect any part of 
the body, such as the lymph nodes, pleura, urogenital system, brain or spine (extrapulmonary 
tuberculosis). Extrapulmonary tuberculosis can occur alone or together with pulmonary tuberculosis. 
In comparison with people without HIV, people with HIV have higher rates of extrapulmonary 
tuberculosis including mycobacteraemia (tuberculosis bloodstream infection) [Pai 2016; Shivakoti 
2017]. Moreover, people with HIV have a higher risk than people without HIV for disseminated 
tuberculosis disease (i.e., affecting multiple organ systems) [Kerkhoff 2017]. In this review, our 
target condition is any form of tuberculosis disease in people with HIV.  
Multiple factors contribute to diagnostic delays and the consequent high mortality among people 
with HIV. The unique pathophysiology and clinical manifestations of tuberculosis in people with HIV 
make diagnosis particularly difficult. First, signs and symptoms are often non-specific such as fever, 
weight loss, and fatigue that may mimic other opportunistic infections like pneumocystis 
pneumonia, histoplasmosis, and non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection, or diseases like HIV-
associated cancers. Cough may or may not be present. Signs and symptoms of extrapulmonary 
tuberculosis are related to the structures affected [Kohli 2021b]. Second, people with HIV with 
compromised immune system may present with paucibacillary pulmonary (low bacterial burden) or 
disseminated tuberculosis disease, which reduces diagnostic yield in biological samples like sputum 
[Hanrahan 2014]. Third, sputum can be difficult to obtain, particularly in people with HIV [Broger 
2023b] and cannot be used to diagnose extrapulmonary tuberculosis.  
The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that people with HIV who report any of cough, 
fever, weight loss, and night sweats (i.e. the WHO four-symptom screen) should be evaluated 
further for tuberculosis [WHO 2021]. In people with HIV, other recommended tuberculosis disease 
screening tools include chest X-ray, C-reactive protein (CRP) and molecular WHO-recommended 
Rapid Diagnostics (mWRD) [WHO 2021].    
Tuberculosis diagnosis historically relies heavily on evaluation of sputum to directly detect presence 
of mycobacteria or mycobacterial components in the specimen. Low complexity automated nucleic 
acid amplification tests (LC-aNAATs) on respiratory samples are part of the mWRD widely used to 
diagnose tuberculosis [World Health Organization. 2021c]. However, this approach may lack 
sensitivity in people unable to produce sputum, with paucibacillary pulmonary tuberculosis or 
extrapulmonary disease, which are more common in people with HIV than those without. A non-
sputum-based diagnostic test that identifies tuberculosis at any location in the body is therefore 
highly desired to improve tuberculosis case detection in people with HIV and ensure timely 
treatment [WHO 2014]. Desired test characteristics includes minimal or non-invasive sample 
collection, short time to result (under one hour), and ability to implement the test without need for 
special equipment, electricity, or sample preparation [WHO 2014]. The antigen-based lateral flow 
urine lipoarabinomannan (LF-LAM) test is recommended by WHO to assist in diagnosing tuberculosis 
among people with HIV [World Health Organization. 2019]. The LF-LAM test is conducted on urine 
and categorizes patients as having ‘tuberculosis disease’ or ‘not tuberculosis disease’, thus agnostic 
to the site of tuberculosis disease.  
While LF-LAM was found to have limited sensitivity in previous reviews [Shah 2016a; Bjerrum 
2019c], the test has advantages of being performed on urine (a relatively easy specimen for 
collection) and capable of being conducted at the point-of-care without electricity or special 
equipment. Moreover, in contrast to a sputum-based testing strategy, LF-LAM test sensitivity 
increases inversely with the degree of immunosuppression [Lawn 2017b; J. Peter 2015b; J.G. Peter 
2016; Shah 2014; Nakiyingi 2014; Bjerrum 2015].
