1. BACKGROUND   
1.1 Target condition being diagnosed  
Of the approximately 10 million annual tuberculosis cases worldwide, an estimated 1.2 million occur in 
children [World Health Organization 2022a]. Tuberculosis remains among the top ten causes of 
mortality in children, accounting for 14% and 11% of tuberculosis-related deaths in HIV-negative people 
and people  with HIV, respectively, largely due to missed diagnosis [World Health Organization 2022a, 
2018]. Especially children <5 years and immunocompromised children are at greatest risk of tuberculosis 
and tuberculosis -related mortality. 
The clinical presentation of tuberculosis is highly non-specific and symptoms overlap with various other 
common paediatric diseases, especially in CHIV [Marais 2006]. Radiographic investigations (i.e., chest 
radiography, CXR) suffer from low sensitivity and specificity as well as a high inter- and intra-observer 
variability, and its use in children is heavily dependent on experienced readers [Du Toit 2002; Swingler 
2005].  
Tuberculosis in children tends to be low in bacterial load (i.e., paucibacillary), resulting in a limited 
sensitivity of available microbiological tests, especially culture [DiNardo 2016]. In addition, children 
often cannot produce sputum and sample collection methods thus frequently require an elaborate 
infrastructure [Shingadia 2003; Kunkel 2016]. In children with HIV (CHIV), whom are at higher risk of 
infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and progression to tuberculosis disease, 
microbiological diagnosis is even more infrequent [A. W. Kay 2021; A. W. Kay 2020]. Hence, sampling is 
often not attempted, especially at lower levels of health care.  
Diagnosis is further hampered by the presentation of disease. Tuberculosis disease presentation varies 
considerably between ages, but is also heavily dependent on comorbidities such HIV and malnutrition 
[Edwards 2007]. Intrathoracic manifestations, meaning either of the lungs or lymph nodes, are the most 
common form of tuberculosis disease in children [Thomas 2019]. 
Molecular WHO (World Health Organization)-recommended rapid diagnostic tests (mWRD) should be 
used as the initial test for diagnosis of tuberculosis in children. Every effort should be made to establish 
bacteriological confirmation and a range of specimen types are recommended by the WHO for initial 
testing using mWRD (see Figure 7) [World Health Organization 2022b]. In addition, the antigen-based 
lateral flow urine lipoarabinomannan (LF-LAM) test is recommended by WHO to assist in diagnosing 
tuberculosis in CHIV who have tuberculosis symptoms or advanced HIV [World Health Organization 
2019].
