5.2 Parallel testing for diagnosis of tuberculosis compared to respiratory LC-
aNAAT alone  
Against the microbiological reference standard, using a parallel test strategy was estimated to increase 
sensitivity by 6.7% (95% CrI 3.8 to 10.7), with a decrease in specificity of -6.8% (95% CrI -9.5 to -4.7), 
compared to respiratory LC-aNAAT alone. Against the composite reference standard, the difference in 
sensitivity for a parallel test strategy was an increase of 16.0% (95% CrI 10.7 to 22.9) while the 
difference in specificity was a decrease of 3.5% (95% CrI -6.6 to -1.7), compared to respiratory LC-aNAAT 
alone.  
Median tuberculosis prevalence based on composite reference standard increased to 33.5% (from 
median of 18.7% based on microbiological reference standard), highlighting major differences between 
the two reference standards. The microbiological reference standard might have missed people with 
tuberculosis, as microbiological confirmation of disease is challenging particularly in people with HIV. 
Immunocompromised patients are more likely to have extrapulmonary tuberculosis disease or 
paucibacillary pulmonary disease that may exist concurrently with disseminated tuberculosis, where a 
sputum-based microbiological reference standard will be compromised. On the other hand, a composite 
reference standard including clinical judgement and based on tuberculosis treatment might have 
wrongly classified participants as tuberculosis positive. Notably, in most studies, clinicians were not 
blinded to the respiratory LC-aNAAT or LF-LAM test results, such that clinical decisions (and 
categorization of participants as having tuberculosis) may have been informed by the index test results, 
leading to incorporation bias. These factors may explain the lower estimated reduction in specificity 
with the parallel testing strategy when using a composite reference standard compared to a 
microbiological reference standard (-3.5% vs -6.8%, respectively).  The estimated gain in sensitivity of
