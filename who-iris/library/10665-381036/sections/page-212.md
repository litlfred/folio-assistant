Composite reference standard 
We defined a composite reference standard as a positive culture or a clinical decision to initiate 
treatment for TB (i.e., clinically diagnosed TB). The composite reference standard may be based on 
the results of microbiological tests, culture or NAATs other than the index test being evaluated; 
imaging studies; histology; and clinical characteristics, and include at least one component test that is 
positive, according to the definition of the primary study authors. Clinical characteristics may include 
cough longer than two weeks, fever, or weight loss; pneumonia that did not improve with antibiotics; 
or a history of close contact who had TB. In the absence of information on TB treatment we accepted 
a study specific definition (i.e., a definition of confirmed TB defined by the primary study authors), if 
available.  
Data collection and analysis 
Four review authors (JD/MKS/VA/AB) in two pairs independently assessed studies for eligibility. Five 
review authors in pairs (LR/ JD/MKS/VA/AB) independently extracted data using a standardized form 
and assessed study quality using the QUADAS-2 tool. Our unit of analysis was per person rather than 
per sample. If a study reported sample level data, we requested person level data from the study 
authors. When such data were unavailable or authors did not respond, if the number of samples was 
close to the number of participants, we included the study because most participants each contributed 
one sample. We performed meta-analysis using a bivariate model to estimate summary sensitivity and 
specificity. We investigated potential sources of heterogeneity by smear and HIV status. 
Assessment of certainty of the evidence 
We assessed the certainty of the evidence using the GRADE approach for reviews of diagnostic 
accuracy studies.  We made judgments separately for sensitivity and specificity. We applied GRADE 
as recommended (Schünemann 2020a; Schünemann 2020b). 
Results  
We identified 2,806 research articles for screening from searches of electronic sources. After de-
duplication, we screened the titles and abstracts of 1,138 unique articles and excluded 988. We 
identified 7 records through bibliography mining and WHO’s public call for data.  We performed full-
text screening of all 151 articles and excluded 122 studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria. 
(Figure 1). We included 29 studies: 27 studies for pulmonary TB and three studies for extrapulmonary 
TB. One of the studies evaluated both pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB.
