6. Author’s conclusions 
6.1 Implications for practice  
Almost half of the estimated tuberculosis cases in children and two-thirds among children less than 
5 years old are not reported to public health programs worldwide [World Health Organization 2023]. 
Underdiagnosis is the main contributor to this case detection gap, and leads to delays in treatment 
initiation [World Health Organization 2023]. As most children who die from tuberculosis never 
received treatment, there is a global need to increase detection of tuberculosis disease in children. 
Consequently, approaches and tools that increase sensitivity have been prioritized over potential 
losses of specificity and risk of overtreatment, as treatment is generally well tolerated and most 
children that die of tuberculosis do so undiagnosed [World Health Organization 2023]. Although 
children are frequently diagnosed clinically based on signs and symptoms, there are also several 
potential benefits to microbiological confirmation of TB disease, including faster initiation of 
treatment and assessment of drug-resistance [Wobudeya 2022]. 
Parallel LC-aNAAT with respiratory and stool samples increased sensitivity, and so may be of value to 
increase case detection. Stool collection is non-invasive and simple stool processing methods have 
been introduced to support LC-aNAAT in community-based settings. However, in these settings, 
collection of respiratory specimens such as induced sputum or gastric aspirates may not be available 
for children. Given the greater increase in sensitivity in parallel testing compared to LC-aNAAT stool, 
further work is needed to explore the benefits and barriers of increasing local capacity or referral for 
collection and LC-aNAAT on respiratory samples. The benefits of parallel testing may be greater in 
high tuberculosis prevalence settings, while there may be a larger proportion of false positive results 
and greater risk of overtreatment in low tuberculosis prevalence areas. This is similar to the current 
WHO recommendation to consider repeat molecular testing in children if the pre-test probability of 
pulmonary TB is 5% or more [World Health Organization 2024]. 
Among CHIV, urine LF-LAM testing provides a non-invasive, point-of-care test for tuberculosis. LF-
LAM is currently recommended as an add-on test, and parallel testing provided an additional 
increase in sensitivity over combined LC-aNAAT respiratory and stool testing. CHIV have further 
challenges in microbiological confirmation, and when children with culture-negative TB disease were 
included in the CRS, the incremental increase in sensitivity was larger when LF-LAM was added. 
However, there was a larger reduction in specificity, and previous studies on LF-LAM on children 
have raised concern for cross-reaction and false positive results [M.P. Nicol 2014; LaCourse 2018].  
CHIV are at higher risk of mortality from tuberculosis, especially those with adnamced HIV disease 
and low CD4 count, and so the greater detection of cases needs to be balanced with overtreatment. 
Similar to parallel LC-aNAAT, the net benefit may be greater in higher prevalence tuberculosis 
settings. The increase in sensitivity was greater when LC-aNAAT was added to LF-LAM. Although a 
complementary test, LF-LAM may be the only test available in settings without access to LC-aNAAT, 
and so similar to stool-based testing, further consideration is needed to assess the feasibility and 
benefit to build local capacity or referral for LC-aNAAT. 
Thus, overall LF-LAM and LC-aNAAT on stool complement LC-aNAAT on respiratory specimens for 
children, and can support greater sensitivity and more detection of pulmonary TB in children. This 
needs to be balanced with the loss of specificity and risk of overtreatment, and may be informed by 
the TB prevalence in the setting.
