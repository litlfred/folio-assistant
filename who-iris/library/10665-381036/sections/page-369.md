to routine tuberculosis diagnostic testing with LC-aNAAT without LF-LAM.  
None of the included trials were primarily designed to evaluate the impact of parallel use of LC-aNAAT 
and LF-LAM, and instead were focused on the impact of diagnostic strategies that included LF-LAM (and 
urine Xpert MTB/RIF in [Gupta-Wright 2018]) compared to those that did not include LF-LAM, and each 
differed in other implementation components (e.g., bedside versus laboratory testing, reporting of 
results to clinical teams, study directed recommendations for treatment). The [Peter 2016] trial design 
included bedside study-directed LF-LAM testing with study-directed recommendations for test 
interpretation and treatment initiation. The [Gupta-Wright 2018] included an intervention of both urine 
LC-aNAAT and urine LF-LAM performed in the laboratory, with clinicians blinded to specific test results 
and told whether tuberculosis had been diagnosed by the laboratory-based testing or not. By contrast, 
Åhsberg 2023 was a cluster-randomized step-wedge study in which urine LF-LAM usage and 
interpretation in intervention clusters was at the discretion of non-study clinicians, and notably only 
46% of the participants with a positive LF-LAM result initiated tuberculosis treatment. The three trials 
took place over differing time periods, which may have been affected by temporal changes in clinical 
care strategies and other factors. The [Åhsberg 2023] trial took place partially during the COVID-19 
pandemic, with imbalances noted in participant characteristics between intervention and control 
clusters, which may have contributed to higher mortality in the intervention group.  
The three included trials were all conducted in African countries, limiting applicability to settings outside 
Africa, although the study sites in the trials span five countries with differing levels of resource 
constraints that may reflect settings elsewhere. The lack of data from outpatient settings also limits the 
directness of the findings to hospital settings. The only outpatient trial with data on the impact of LF-
LAM could not be included in this systematic review since LC-aNAAT was not included as part of the 
study procedures [Grant 2020].  
Our prior systematic review on the impact of diagnostic strategies using LF-LAM on mortality reported a 
pooled risk ratio of 0.85 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.94) indicating that the use of LF-LAM likely reduces mortality 
in adult inpatients with HIV, based on data from two trials [J.G. Peter 2016; Gupta-Wright 2018].  This 
contrasts with the current review that includes an additional trial [Åhsberg 2023], with heterogeneity 
between individual study effect estimates. Given noted differences in diagnostic test implementation 
strategies and study design differences, these pooled results should be interpreted cautiously. 
Moreover, since mortality is influenced by multiple factors including timing of presentation to a health 
facility (which impacts disease severity) and how and whether clinicians act on test results (or the lack 
thereof), understanding the effects of parallel testing on key process outcomes earlier in the diagnostic 
testing cascade is also important for decision makers. This review suggests that parallel testing with LF-
LAM and LC-aNAAT likely increase the proportion of inpatients with HIV who are diagnosed with 
tuberculosis and probably increases the proportion who are treated for tuberculosis.
