Authors’ conclusion  
Respiratory LC-aNAAT and urine LF-LAM may be complementary when performed as part of a 
parallel testing strategy to diagnose all forms of HIV-associated tuberculosis. Reasons for this 
observed increase in diagnostic sensitivity with addition of urine LF-LAM could be its ease of use, 
ability to be performed on non-sputum samples (for patients unable to produce sputum), and ability 
to identify extrapulmonary or disseminated forms of tuberculosis as a non-site-specific test, despite 
relatively modest individual sensitivity.   
Parallel testing improves sensitivity at a cost of reduced specificity compared to LC-aNAAT alone. 
The gain in sensitivity should be weighed against the loss of specificity, in settings with varying 
tuberculosis prevalence. Especially in low-prevalence settings, using the tests in parallel may lead to 
a large increase in false positive results whereas in high-prevalence settings with limited patient 
follow-up and extrapulmonary tuberculosis investigation, the benefit of identifying additional 
patients with tuberculosis at the point-of-care likely outweighs the relatively lower risk of 
overtreatment of others. In this review, we found that more patients in a Sub-Saharan Africa 
inpatient setting were diagnosed with tuberculosis and initiated on treatment when a parallel 
strategy compared with respiratory LC-aNAAT alone was available. Evidence regarding the impact of 
parallel testing on mortality were conflicting and may have been a result of study heterogeneity; 
further investigation is necessary to improve certainty regarding the effect of parallel testing. 
Implementation research is critical to understand how tests and testing strategies are used in 
different routine clinical settings.
