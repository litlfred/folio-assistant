3.1.5 Reference standards 
We required studies to ascertain the presence or absence of tuberculosis disease using at least one 
of the following two reference standards:  
3.1.5.1 Microbiological Reference Standard 
For a microbiological reference standard  
• Presence of tuberculosis disease was defined as a positive M. tuberculosis culture on 
any sample type or a positive NAAT result for detection of tuberculosis on any 
sample other than LC-aNAAT on respiratory samples. 
• Absence of tuberculosis disease was defined as negative M. tuberculosis cultures and 
negative NAAT performed on any sample type other than LC-aNAAT on respiratory 
samples. 
We included studies that used either culture, NAAT (other than respiratory LC-aNAAT), or a parallel 
combination of both (in which positivity was defined as either test positive) as microbiological 
reference standard. We excluded respiratory LC-aNAAT and urine LF-LAM test results from the 
microbiological reference standard to avoid incorporation bias. 
We expected all studies to obtain sputum culture(s) and some studies to obtain additional 
specimens from other body sites for culture or LC-aNAAT. Based on current WHO recommendations 
and clinical practice, Xpert on extrapulmonary samples (Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), urine, stool) is also 
sometimes undertaken for the purpose of categorizing tuberculosis. We included non-respiratory 
NAAT to improve the microbiological reference standard as there are risks of misclassification bias to 
ignore these extrapulmonary microbiological tests given the target condition is both pulmonary and 
extrapulmonary TB.  
Some studies relied on sputum culture alone as microbiological reference standard with the risk of 
tuberculosis disease being missed for the following reasons: the population of people with HIV may 
be characterized by low quality sputum specimens; paucibacillary pulmonary disease; sputum 
scarcity; or extrapulmonary tuberculosis without pulmonary tuberculosis. Consequently, true 
positive index test results may be misclassified as false positive using sputum culture alone as 
reference standard and underestimate specificity for LF-LAM as well as the incremental value of the 
parallel strategy. The sensitivity of the microbiological reference standard can be increased by 
evaluating multiple specimens, including evaluating specimens from extrapulmonary sites of disease. 
If only one specimen was used for culture, we considered the risk of bias to be high in the reference 
standard domain. The quality of the reference standard was addressed in the reference standard 
domain of the revised Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool (See 
section 3.3.3) 
3.1.5.2 Composite Reference Standard 
We additionally included studies using a composite reference standard: 
• Presence of tuberculosis disease was defined as a positive microbiological reference 
standard as defined above or positive composite reference standard.  
• Absence of tuberculosis disease was defined as negative microbiological reference 
standard and a negative composite reference standard. 
The composite reference standard might be based on the results of microbiological tests, culture or 
NAAT; imaging studies; histology; and clinical characteristics, and include at least one component 
test that is positive, according to the definition of the primary study authors. Criteria may include 
persisting symptoms (e.g. cough longer than two weeks, fever, night sweats, or weight loss), 
radiographic findings consistent with tuberculosis, positive sputum smear microscopy, or clinical 
decision to start tuberculosis treatment.
