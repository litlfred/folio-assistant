Background 
Description of the condition 
TB is one of the leading causes of death worldwide and affects all ages (1). TB is caused by 
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) bacteria. TB primarily affects the respiratory 
system causing pulmonary TB (PTB) but can also affect other organ systems in the form of 
extrapulmonary TB (EPTB). While TB is preventable and largely curable, an estimated 10.6 
million people fell ill with TB worldwide in 2022 and around 1.3 million people died of TB (2, 
3). 
 
Pulmonary TB 
PTB is the most prevalent form of TB. Loss of appetite, weight loss, lethargy, fever, chills, night 
sweats, cough, and haemoptysis are common symptoms of PTB. The natural history of TB in 
children is distinct from that in adults, due to more frequent progression to primary TB 
disease (4). Children and adolescents are at a higher risk for progression to the disease 
following infection with M. tuberculosis, with increased susceptibility amongst the under-five 
age group. The clinical spectrum of TB in children may range from asymptomatic primary 
infection, symptomatic TB disease, and extrapulmonary manifestations to disseminated TB or 
adult-type disease (5). Older children and adolescents more commonly manifest adult-type 
diseases, including pleural TB and upper lobe consolidations (4). 
 
Extrapulmonary TB 
EPTB is known to affect virtually every part of the body, with lymph nodes and the pleura 
being the most common sites (6). Although active PTB is transmissible by droplets spread by 
coughing, EPTB is thought to result from haematogenous spread (spread by way of the 
bloodstream) from an initial lung infection and is not infectious. EPTB can occur alone or 
together with PTB. The various forms of EPTB cause signs and symptoms related to the 
structures affected.
