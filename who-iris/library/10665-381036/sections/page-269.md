We included studies using a CRS consisting of a combination of microbiological tests and clinical criteria, 
including M. tuberculosis culture, NAATs on any sample type (pulmonary and extrapulmonary), or 
clinical criteria specified by the primary study authors. We included NAATs on non-respiratory sample 
types as children who have pulmonary tuberculosis often also have extrapulmonary TB. Clinical criteria 
to classify tuberculosis was defined by each study, and may be based on imaging studies, histology, 
clinical characteristics, and treatment response. Clinical characteristics may include cough longer than 
two weeks, fever, or weight loss; pneumonia that did not improve with antibiotics; or a history of close 
contact who had TB. Combined with the MRS definition, the CRS was defined as: 
• 
Presence of pulmonary tuberculosis disease if positive by the MRS or meeting author-defined 
clinical criteria, including LC-aNAATs on any sample type listed above.  
• 
Absence of pulmonary tuberculosis disease if negative by the MRS and not meeting author-
defined clinical criteria. 
The CRS classification may incorporate results from one or more index tests (LF-LAM, LC-aNAATs, or 
both); for example, the decision to start tuberculosis treatment may have been informed by the results 
of LC-aNAAT or LAM. This will likely lead to overestimation of diagnostic accuracy. If the reference 
standard incorporated any of the index tests, we considered the risk of bias to be high.    
With the uncertainties surrounding a clinical diagnosis, the CRS may incorrectly classify people without 
tuberculosis as having tuberculosis disease. If people are correctly classified as negative by the index test 
but incorrectly classified as positive by the CRS, this will lead to an underestimation of the index tests’ 
sensitivity.  
We performed as separate risk-of-bias assessments for the MRS and CRS, respectively. In absence of any 
other risk of bias issues, we rated the risk of bias ’unclear’ if the CRS involved clinical features to confirm 
tuberculosis. This is consistent with the 2016 review which also included CRS [Shah 2016b]. 
3.2 Search methods for identification of studies 
We attempted to identify all relevant studies regardless of language or publication status (published, 
unpublished, in press, and ongoing).  
3.2.1 Electronic searches  
The Cochrane Infectious Diseases Group Information Specialist developed and performed the literature 
search on November 3rd, 2023, using the search strategy described in Appendix 4. We searched the 
following electronic databases: 
• 
Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL)  
• 
MEDLINE (OVID)  
• 
Embase (OVID)  
• 
Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-EXPANDED), Conference Proceedings Citation Index- 
Science (CPCI-S), and BIOSIS Previews, all three using the Web of Science platform  
• 
Scopus (Elseviers) 
• 
WHO Global Index Medicus  
• 
Clinicaltrials.gov and the search portal of the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform 
(WHO ICTRP, www.who.int/trialsearch) for ongoing trials  
• 
ProQuest Dissertations & Theses A&l for relevant dissertations.  
We did not use search filters for diagnostic accuracy studies [Beynon 2013]. We limited the search to 
studies published published after 2010; Xpert MTB/RIF was first endorsed in 2010 for clinical use and 
other LC-aNAATs and LF-LAM first became available several years later.
