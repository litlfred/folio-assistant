4.2.2 QUADAS-C 
Figure 10 shows risk of bias for 31 studies comparing a parallel use of LC-aNAAT on respiratory 
samples and LF-LAM on urine to LC-aNAAT on respiratory samples alone. All studies for the purpose 
of this review used a paired diagnostic accuracy design.  
In the patient selection domain, we considered 21 studies (68%) to have low risk of bias. We 
considered 10 studies (32%) to have high risk of bias as the risk of patient selection bias for both 
index tests was considered high mainly because studies excluded participants that could not 
produce samples for testing [Benjamin 2019; Bjerrum 2015; Broger-Cohort4 2020; de Vasconcellos 
2021; Kebede 2021; J. Peter 2015a; J.G. Peter 2016; Shah 2014; Shapiro 2022; Stead 2023]  
In the index test domain, we considered all studies (100%) to have low risk of bias as the risk of bias 
for each index test was judged low per QUADAS-2. We also thought that the LF-LAM (when 
performed on fresh urine) was interpreted without knowledge of that of LC-aNAAT and as the result 
of LC-aNAAT is automatic there would be no need of blinding to LF-LAM. When LF-LAM was 
performed on frozen samples, studies sufficiently described that the result was blinded to that of 
the LC-aNAAT results. We found it unlikely that conduction and interpretation of one index test 
would have unwanted effects or advantages on subsequent index tests.  
In the microbiological reference standard domain, we considered 15 studies (56%) to have low risk 
of bias. We considered 10 studies (37%) to have high risk of bias as we judged the reference 
standard likely to misclassify participants for LF-LAM and LC-aNAAT in QUADAS 2 and therefore also 
their comparison [Bjerrum 2015; Broger-Cohort4 2020; Calligaro 2017; Gils 2023; Gupta-Wright 
2018; Kasaro 2020; Kebede 2021; J. Peter 2015a; Spooner 2022; Yoon 2019]. Two studies (7%) had 
unclear risk of bias for the comparison as it was unclear if the reference standard for the index test 
was likely to classify the participants correct [Liu 2022; Shapiro 2022]. All studies avoided 
incorporating LC-aNAAT and LF-LAM as part of the reference standard.   
In the composite reference standard domain, we considered 22 studies (96%) to have high risk of 
bias: 21 studies as the risk of bias for the index test was considered high in QUADAS-2 and 21 studies 
incorporated either LF-LAM, Lc-aNAAT or both in the reference standard to classify participants as 
tuberculosis cases. We considered one study (4%) to have unclear risk of bias for the comparison as 
the risk of bias for this domain was unclear in QUADAS 2 [Broger-Cohort4 2020]. 
In the flow and timing domain, we considered nine studies (29%) to have high risk of bias: six studies 
because they had high risk of bias for the individual test in QUADAS 2  [Gupta-Wright 2018; Huerga 
2017a; Huerga 2021; Liu 2022; Spooner 2022; Stead 2023], one study because there was not an 
appropriate interval between tests (Liu), six studies because the same reference standard was not 
used across index test groups comparison [Gupta-Wright 2018; Huerga 2023a; Huerga 2017a; 
Huerga 2021; Liu 2022; Spooner 2022], eight studies because the proportions of unavailable test 
results differed considerably between index test  [Gupta-Wright 2018; Huerga 2023a; Huerga 2017a; 
Huerga 2021; Huerga 2020; Liu 2022; Stead 2023; Lawn 2017b]. Test results were unavailable mainly 
because of inability of participants to produce samples and in particular sputum, but could also 
include test with non-determinate test results for LF-LAM or invalid LC-aNAAT although these were 
often reported to be low. We considered one study to have unclear risk of bias in comparison with a 
parallel strategy and LC-aNAAT alone [de Vasconcellos 2021].
