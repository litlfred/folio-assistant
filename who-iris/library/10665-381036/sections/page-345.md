In the index test domain, we judged that all studies had low risk of bias for both index tests. For LC-
aNAAT the results of the index tests are automatically generated. For LF-LAM all included studies 
pre-specified the grade used for positivity and interpreted the test at the recommended positivity 
threshold without knowledge of the results of the reference standard. Regarding applicability, with 
respect to LC-aNAAT there were no concern. For LF-LAM, four studies were judged to have unclear 
concern: three as stored urine samples were tested [Boloko 2022; Broger-Cohort4 2020; Garcia 
2020], and for one study we could not tell [Åhsberg 2023].  
In the reference standard domain, 27 studies evaluated diagnostic accuracy against a microbiological 
reference standard, 23 studies against a composite reference standard and 20 studies included both.  
For a microbiological reference standard, we considered 13 studies (48%) to have low risk of bias 
because the studies attempted to collect sputum for culture and specimens from different sites for 
extrapulmonary tuberculosis (e.g. urine for Xpert) and the reference standard was interpreted 
without knowledge of the results of the index test. We considered 11 studies (41%) to be at high risk 
of bias: nine studies did not include testing of any extrapulmonary specimens [Bjerrum 2015; Broger-
Cohort4 2020; Calligaro 2017; Gils 2023; Huerga 2019; Kasaro 2020; Kebede 2021; J. Peter 2015a; 
Yoon 2019], and one study did not have a standardized approach for sputum culture [Gupta-Wright 
2018] and one study did not have a standardized approach for testing extrapulmonary samples 
[Spooner 2022]. We considered three studies (11%) to have unclear risk of bias: one study included a 
positive blood Xpert Ultra as part of tuberculosis cases [Boloko 2022] and for two studies the 
approach for testing extrapulmonary samples was unclear [Liu 2022; Shapiro 2022].  
For a composite reference standard, 21 (91%) studies had high risk of bias: 16 studies involved 
clinical judgement to confirm tuberculosis were it was unclear if this correctly classified participants 
and the studies interpreted the reference standard with knowledge of the index test results 
[Åhsberg 2023; Boloko 2022; Bonnet 2023; Calligaro 2017; de Vasconcellos 2021; Huerga 2023a; 
Huerga 2017a; Huerga 2020; Kasaro 2020; Olbrich 2023; J. Peter 2015a; J.G. Peter 2016; Shapiro 
2022; Stead 2023; Szekely 2022; Van Hoving 2019] (; two studies had blinded interpretation of the 
reference standard to the index test results, but involved clinical judgement [Bjerrum 2015; Gupta-
Wright 2018], and in three studies interpretation of the reference standard was not blinded to index 
test [Bjerrum 2015; Huerga 2021; Spooner 2022]. One study had unclear risk of bias as the reference 
standard included clinical judgement and it was unclear if blinding to index tests was performed 
[Broger-Cohort4 2020]. Regarding applicability of the reference standard, we thought that 27 studies 
(87%) had low concern because these studies performed a test to identify M tuberculosis species 
(speciation) for the microbiological reference standard, had an attempt by design to ensure a 
minimum of standard testing and appropriate criteria for composite reference standard. Two studies 
(6%) had high concern as they lacked protocol directed testing of either sputum culture [Gupta-
Wright 2018] or both of sputum and extrapulmonary samples [Liu 2022]. One study had unclear 
concern because we could not tell whether the study performed speciation [Kasaro 2020].
