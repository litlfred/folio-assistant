EXCECUTIVE SUMMARY  
Background  
Low complexity automated nucleic acid amplification tests (LC-aNAATs) are part of the molecular 
World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended rapid diagnostics widely used to diagnose 
tuberculosis disease. The lateral flow urine lipoarabinomannan (LF-LAM) test is recommended by 
WHO to assist in diagnosing tuberculosis disease among people with HIV. Previous systematic 
reviews have traditionally assessed diagnostic accuracy of LC-aNAATs and LF-LAM tests each in 
isolation for detection of tuberculosis, but in clinical practice the tests may be used in parallel (i.e. 
LF-LAM in combination with LC-aNAAT). 
Objective  
We conducted a systematic review to compare the diagnostic accuracy of the parallel use of LC-
aNAATs on respiratory samples and LF-LAM on urine versus each test alone for detection of 
tuberculosis disease in adults and adolescents with HIV presenting with presumptive tuberculosis. 
We define parallel use as follows: both tests are conducted simultaneously on the same person, and 
a positive result on either test implies a positive result for the combination. 
We also assessed the effect of implementing a parallel testing strategy on patient-important 
outcomes, compared to a strategy with respiratory LC-aNAAT alone. 
Search methods  
We searched the Cochrane Infectious Disease Group Specialized Register, MEDLINE, Embase, 
Science Citation Index, WHO Global Index Medicus, ClinicalTrial.gov, and the WHO International 
Clinical Trials Registry up to November 3rd, 2023.   
Selection criteria 
We included studies that allowed assessment of the accuracy of both parallel testing and one of the 
component tests in the same study group against a microbiological or composite reference standard.  
Data collection and analysis 
Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form and assessed study quality 
using QUADAS-2 and QUADAS-C tools. We performed bivariate random-effects meta-analysis using a 
Bayesian approach to estimate sensitivity and specificity and absolute differences between index 
tests. We performed subgroup analyses by presence of signs and symptoms; CD4 cell count, and 
clinical setting as well as separate analyses for those with screening positivity for tuberculosis, 
advanced HIV, and serious illness.  
Main results for comparative diagnostic accuracy  
In 27 studies involving 12,651 participants, of whom 2,368 (18.7%) had tuberculosis based on a 
microbiological reference standard, parallel use of respiratory LC-aNAAT and urine LF-LAM had a 
pooled sensitivity and specificity (95% credible interval) of 77.5% (73.4 to 81.3) and 89.4% (85.8 to 
92.3). Compared to respiratory LC-aNAAT alone, parallel testing had 6.7 (3.8 to 10.7) percentage 
points higher sensitivity and -6.8 (-9.5 to -4.7) percentage points lower specificity. In 23 studies, 
11,109 participants, of whom 3,723 (33.5%) had tuberculosis based on a composite reference 
standard, pooled sensitivity and specificity of parallel testing was of 67.6% (59.9 to 74.6) and 96.2% 
(92.8 to 98.1). Compared to respiratory LC-aNAAT alone, parallel testing had 16.0 (10.7 to 22.9) 
percentage points higher sensitivity and -3.5 (-6.6 to -1.7) percentage points lower specificity, using 
a composite reference standard.  
Main results for impact on patient important outcomes 
Three randomized trials in inpatient settings assessed the impact of diagnostic strategies that 
utilized LC-aNAAT and LF-LAM for clinical decision-making in people with HIV on mortality, and 
clinical outcomes related to diagnosis and treatment. In inpatient settings, parallel testing 
(respiratory LC-aNAAT and urine LF-LAM) has an uncertain effect on mortality (risk ratio 0.93; 95% CI 
0.74 to 1.17) at eight weeks in people with HIV compared to routine tuberculosis diagnostic testing
