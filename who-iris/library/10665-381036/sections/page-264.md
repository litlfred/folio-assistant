1.2.2 Index test, LF-LAM 
Urine LF-LAM is a commercially available point-of-care test for tuberculosis disease. Lipoarabinomannan 
(LAM) is a lipopolysaccharide present in mycobacterial cell walls, which is released from metabolically 
active or degenerating bacterial cells during tuberculosis disease [Briken 2004]. LAM is detectable in 
urine of people with active tuberculosis disease and can be detected with both LAM ELISA requiring 
laboratory facilities and point-of-care LF-LAM test strips ready to use at the patient’s bedside [Minion 
2011; Shah 2016b]. LF-LAM testing is performed manually by applying 60 μL of unprocessed urine to the 
sample pad of the Determine TB LAM Ag test and leave the strip test to incubate at room temperature 
for 25 minutes [Abbott]. If a control band is visible by the eye, the test is valid and the intensity of the 
patient band is visually compared with the intensity of the manufacturer-supplied reference scale card. 
A 2019 updated Cochrane review assessed the accuracy of a single LF-LAM test for the diagnosis of 
active pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis in adult PLHIV [Bjerrum 2019b]. Among symptomatic 
patients, the LF-LAM pooled sensitivity was 42% (95% credible interval (CrI): 31% to 55%) and pooled 
specificity was 91% (CrI: 85% to 95%) (8 studies, 3449 participants, 37% with pulmonary or 
extrapulmonary tuberculosis).   
In children, a recent meta-analysis pooled data from 2,076 children that underwent LF-LAM testing in 
nine studies and estimated a sensitivity of 16% and a specificity of 96% [Seid 2022]. In CHIV, LF-LAM 
sensitivity and specificity were estimated at 47% and 76%, respectively. The LF-LAM specificity is still not 
completely understood and factors such as an incomplete reference standard [Bjerrum 2019b], test 
misinterpretation [Mwaura 2021; Mathabire Rucker 2019], cross reactivity within the mycobacterial 
family [Qvist 2014; Nel 2017], and contaminators among diaper-wearing children may play a role [M.P. 
Nicol 2014; Kroidl 2015].  
1.3 Role of the tests 
For LC-aNAAT, the suggested role is an initial test for the diagnosis of tuberculosis in children [World 
Health Organization 2024]. WHO states that “LF-LAM should be used as an add-on to clinical judgement 
in combination with other tests. It should not be used as a replacement or triage test” [World Health 
Organization. 2021]. The LF-LAM and LC-aNAAT may be done in parallel (i.e., LF-LAM in combination 
with LC-aNAAT) where a positive result on either test implies a positive result for the combination of 
tests [World Health Organization. 2021]. 
Several studies have highlighted the incremental yield of repeated sampling of respiratory specimens 
[M. P. Nicol 2011; Song 2021], and testing multiple respiratory specimens for tuberculosis is considered 
the standard of care in many parts of the world. In this review, we will assess the incremental accuracy 
of parallel stool-based testing with respiratory sample testing.  
1.4 Rationale 
Several systematic reviews have been conducted to assess the diagnostic accuracy of low-complexity 
WHO recommended molecular rapid diagnostic tests (Xpert MTB/RIF, Xpert Ultra, Truenat MTB, MTB 
Plus,) in adults for both pulmonary samples [Zifodya 2021; Shapiro 2021; A. W. Kay 2020; A.W. Kay 
2022] and extrapulmonary samples [Kohli 2021]. For PLHIV, there are additional systematic reviews and

could be an incremental value in combining the WHO recommended rapid tests (i.e., LF-LAM and
