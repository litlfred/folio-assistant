Study quality may influence estimates of diagnostic accuracy. Comparing parallel testing with respiratory 
LC-aNAAT alone, the increase in sensitivity was highest in studies at low risk of bias in the Patient 
Selection domain (QUADAS-C), namely those that included participants regardless of their inability to 
produce sputum samples [Gupta-Wright 2018; Lawn 2017b; Stead 2023] (see Figure 10). These studies 
had lower estimates of sensitivity for the sputum LC-aNAAT with associated higher incremental 
increases in sensitivity with the addition of LF-LAM in the parallel testing strategy. Conversely, studies 
with a high risk of selection bias (excluding patients unable to produce sputum (or urine)) reported 
limited gain in sensitivity with the addition of LF-LAM as part of a parallel testing strategy, compared to 
respiratory LC-aNAAT alone.  
The reported sensitivities of parallel testing varied across individual studies; similarly, there was 
variability in the reported sensitivities of the component tests (Appendix 13). While we theorized that 
this heterogeneity may be attributable to differences in design, setting, relative degree of 
immunosuppression of included participants, and reference standard, investigations of heterogeneity 
did not provide conclusive evidence of between-subgroup differences in comparative accuracy. The 
overall pooled sensitivity estimate (77.5%) did not vary substantially when considering pre-specified 
subgroups when evaluating heterogeneity (CD4 ≤ 200 78.7%, advanced HIV 81.9%, seriously ill 
individuals 78.7%, inpatients 77.7%, outpatients 76%).     
Similarly, the incremental increase in sensitivity (pooled difference estimate 6.7%) and decline in 
specificity (pooled difference estimate -6.8%) of a parallel testing strategy compared to respiratory LC-
aNAAT alone varied across individual studies. This heterogeneity was hypothesized to be attributable to 
the variability in accuracy of the individual index tests, as well as differences in study design, setting, and 
patient selection including ability to produce sputum for respiratory LC-aNAAT, and reference standard 
testing. Nonetheless, the pooled estimates of the incremental change with parallel testing compared to 
individual testing did not differ substantially across most subgroups.  
Overall, we found that the specificity of parallel testing with the combination of LF-LAM and respiratory 
LC-aNAAT was just below 90%. This estimated specificity was -6.8% lower than LC-aNAAT alone. Across 
individual studies, specificity of the parallel testing strategy was lowest when the microbiological 
reference standard was based on sputum culture alone or with few extrapulmonary samples [Åhsberg 
2023; Huerga 2019] suggesting limitations in the microbiological reference standard. When investigating 
heterogeneity, the decline in specificity for a parallel testing strategy, compared to respiratory LC-aNAAT 
alone was highest for subgroups involving low CD4 count, seriously ill patients, and inpatient settings. 
The change in specificity for parallel testing compared to individual testing also differed between 
analyses of asymptomatic participants (-2.5% decline) compared to symptomatic participants (-6.8%). 
Estimates of specificity for individual and parallel testing are anticipated to be affected by the reference 
standard. This consideration is particularly important when evaluating diagnostic accuracy of urine LF-
LAM and parallel testing strategies, because LF-LAM assays are non-site specific [Bjerrum 2019b]. We 
judge a reference standard as high quality if including multiple specimens from different sites (and 
testing with mycobacterial culture, or LC-aNAAT for non-respiratory samples). We considered the 
potential for misclassification of individuals with tuberculosis when sputum culture was not performed, 
or when extrapulmonary samples were not evaluated. In these instances, misclassification bias could 
lead to lower estimates of test specificity. By contrast, usage of composite reference standard which 
incorporates the test results of the index tests in clinical decision making may lead to overestimates of 
test specificity.
