5.4 Parallel use of LC-aNAAT on respiratory samples plus stool and LF-LAM 
for diagnosis of tuberculosis compared to each assay alone (PICO 2) 
For the parallel use of LC-aNAATS combined and LF-LAM in CHIV, the overall diagnostic accuracy of 
parallel testing was estimated at a sensitivity of 77.6% and a specificity of 83.9% with MRS. 
Sensitivity estimates ranged from 64% to 89% between studies, and similar to the result in PICO 1, 
may reflect that five of the six included studies had less than 10 CHIV each who were culture-
positive, mostly due to low tuberculosis disease prevalence when applying the MRS. Similarly, 
specificity ranged from 63% to 92% between studies, mainly due to large variation in specificity for 
LF-LAM between studies (range 70% to 97%). 
Compared to LC-aNAATs combined, parallel testing including LF-LAM increased sensitivity by an 
absolute value of 6.9% at a loss of specificity at 10.2% with an MRS. The differences reflect the 
added value of LF-LAM testing in CHIV, at a loss of specificity, which may reflect false positive results 
or additional cases of CHIV with respiratory culture-negative tuberculosis disease. In children, 
especially in those with HIV, extrapulmonary tuberculosis disease is common, which might have 
been picked up by the LF-LAM. 
As CHIV are amongst the populations hardest to diagnose, especially when relying on microbiological 
testing alone, diagnosis is often made based on clinical judgement and thus the use of CRS is of 
particular importance. With the CRS, the sensitivity of parallel testing was 30.0% and specificity 
83.3%. Overall, the sensitivity for parallel testing as well as each test alone decreased compared to 
MRS. This reflects that the CRS definition of tuberculosis includes children who were diagnosed 
clinically and could be culture- and LC-aNAAT-negative, which likely includes both children with and 
without tuberculosis disease.  
The sensitivity estimates ranged from 6.0% to 88.0% between studies, with all except for one study 
having a sensitivity below 50.0%. The study with the highest sensitivity of 88.0% was the only study 
to define the CRS based on clinical decision to treat. Specificity ranged from 25.0% to 93.0% 
between studies, with all except one study having a specificity above 70.0%. The study with the 
lowest specificity of 25.0% included no culture-confirmed CHIV (i.e., tuberculosis prevalence of 0% 
per MRS) while reporting the highest tuberculosis prevalence of all studies for CRS (i.e., 80.9%).  
Compared to LC-aNAATs combined, parallel testing including LF-LAM increased sensitivity by an 
absolute value of 11.5% at a loss of specificity at 10.1% against a CRS. While loss of specificity was 
similar, the gain in sensitivity was higher compared to an MRS. The differences reflect the added 
value of LF-LAM testing in CHIV, at a loss of specificity, which may reflect false positive results or 
additional cases of CHIV with respiratory culture-negative tuberculosis disease. For a population 
with a tuberculosis disease prevalence of 40%, this translates to the identification of an additional 46 
cases at the cost of 61 false-positives.  
Similar to parallel testing without LF_LAM, sensitivity as well as specificity varied between studies, 
ranging from 0% to 33.3% for sensitivity and 5.0% to 75.0% for specificity, potentially as a result of 
applying an imperfect reference standard as well as inconsistent definitions for CRS. 
Subgroup analyses were performed for MRS, however, paired comparisons were not possible due to 
scarceness of data. Compared to the primary analyses including all CHIV, those with advanced HIV-
disease had similar sensitivity (77.8% versus 79.3%) and specificity estimates for parallel testing 
(83.9% versus 80.2%), but a lower gain in sensitivity (6.9% versus 1.2%) while the loss of specificity 
remained similar (-10.2% versus -10.5%). The individual test accuracy estimates suggest this to be 
mainly driven by higher sensitivity estimates for LC-aNAAT combined (69.3% versus 78.2%);
