Population  
We included studies about users or potential users of low and moderate complexity NAATs. Users at 
recipient level were children, adolescents, adults and people living with HIV with signs and symptoms of 
TB and/or screened positive for TB or their caregivers. Users at provider level were health care providers 
(clinicians, nurses, laboratory technicians), TB programme officers or managers or other staff involved in 
ordering, maintaining or acting on results of TB diagnostic tests. We did not limit inclusion by race, 
socioeconomic status or geographical setting. 
 Intervention 
 
 
We included studies with provider or recipient views on low complexity and moderate complexity NAATs 
for the detection of TB and drug-resistant TB. Specifically, we included studies with  perceptions on three 
classes of NAATs including, low complexity manual NAATs for detection of TB, low complexity automated 
NAATs for the detection of TB and drug resistance to rifampicin, isoniazid and 2nd line anti TB drugs and 
moderate complexity automated NAATs for detection of resistance of rifampicin and isoniazid and 2nd line 
anti-TB drugs.5 7 We did not include high complexity NAATs due to their high infrastructural requirements 
and limited access to date.  
 
The eligible tests for our review question are detailed in Table 1. We identified studies about views or 
experiences on acceptability, equity, feasibility, implementation and values of the aforementioned 
NAATs. The tests could be conducted on respiratory and non-respiratory samples or a combination 
thereof.
